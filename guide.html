0.1 How to write a full-credit short answer (use this template every time)

  1. State the answer directly in the first sentence (the “what”).
  2. Give the mechanism / causal chain (the “why”). This is where the points are. Prof. Long says over and over: “That’s just a factoid. What I want you to understand is why that is true.”
  3. Use her vocabulary: decompression melting, dehydration (flux) melting, elastic rebound, stick-slip, site amplification, resonance, isostasy, viscosity, volatiles, felsic/mafic, Bowen’s reaction series, lithosphere, slab pull, etc.
  4. Add one example or analogy from class (lava lamp, Silly Putty, slinky, lightning–thunder, toothpaste vs. melted ice cream, shaken soda, salt on an icy sidewalk, beach ball under water, memory foam, molasses in winter).
  5. Compare when asked to compare. Say what is different and why (e.g., “felsic is MORE viscous BECAUSE it is cooler, more silica-rich, and more crystal-rich”).
  6. Draw a quick sketch when it helps (fault blocks, wave motions, phase diagram, convection cell). Sketches earn credit on conceptual exams.

0.2 The six fatal mistakes (she has explicitly warned about each)

❌ Never write ✅ Write instead
“The mantle is liquid / molten.” (She said she would “cry into a double scotch.”) The mantle is solid rock that flows (a fluid, not a liquid) over long timescales, very slowly (1–10 cm/yr). It melts only in a few places beneath volcanoes.
“Subduction-zone magma forms because the slab gets hotter and melts.” Subduction zones are colder than average mantle. Water released from the slab lowers the melting temperature of the overlying mantle wedge (dehydration/flux melting). Nothing was heated.
“Rising mantle melts because it heats up.” Rising mantle cools slightly; it melts because pressure drops, lowering the melting temperature (decompression / pressure-release melting).
“Richter/Mercalli measure the same thing.” Magnitude = size/energy of the earthquake (one number per quake, logarithmic). Mercalli intensity = shaking at a particular place (many values per quake).
“Bigger magnitude always means more deaths.” Damage depends on magnitude and depth, distance, local geology (site amplification), building construction, wave frequency vs. building resonance, warning systems, and how hazard is communicated (Haiti 2010 vs. Chile 2010).
“Hawaii looks like the 🌋 emoji.” The emoji is a stratovolcano (felsic, subduction zone). Hawaii is a broad shield volcano (mafic, hotspot).

UNIT 1 · Energy for disasters and basic physics (Lectures 1–3)

1.1 Official key points

  • There are three major energy sources for natural disasters.
  • All of them ultimately come from the Big Bang and relate to the processes that formed the solar system.
  • Geological disasters ← heat in Earth’s interior (left over from planet formation + radioactive decay).
  • Meteorological disasters ← energy from the Sun (fusion of H into He).
  • Cosmological disasters ← energy in planetary orbits (potential + kinetic).
  • The sources can interact, and tides are the example.
  • Work, energy, force, power; F = ma; units J, N, W; MKS units.
  • Types of energy (potential, kinetic, heat) and energy conservation.
  • Latent vs. sensible heat, and heat capacity.
  • (Sep 9 recorded lecture) scientific notation; precision/significant figures; estimating Earth’s mass.

1.2 The three disaster types and their fuel

Disaster type Examples Energy source Where that energy came from
Geological earthquakes, volcanoes, tsunami Heat from Earth’s interior: 46 TW, about 50% primordial heat + 50% radioactive decay Primordial heat = KE of colliding bodies during accretion turned to heat (PE → KE → heat). Radiogenic heat = decay of U → Pb, Th → Pb, K → Ar; the emitted particles (α, β, γ) are absorbed and become heat.
Meteorological hurricanes, storms, floods, tornadoes, heat waves, climate change The Sun: solar constant 1,360 W/m², total 170,000 TW ≈ 4,000× Earth’s interior output Nuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium (proton–proton reaction) in the Sun’s core, which ignited when gravitational collapse heated the core above 10⁷ K.
Cosmological asteroid/comet impacts Orbital energy = orbital kinetic energy + gravitational potential energy (Earth’s orbit alone ≈ 3 × 10³³ J, “a trillion 100-W bulbs burning for a trillion years”) On impact, orbital KE turns into heat and work (lofting debris; ask the dinosaurs).

Why everything traces back to the Big Bang: all matter and energy were at one point ~14 billion years ago. The solar system formed ~4.6 Gyr ago when a molecular cloud collapsed under gravity (maybe triggered by a supernova shock wave). Gravitational potential energy was converted to kinetic energy and heat. That heat ignited fusion in the Sun, gave the planets their orbital energy, and left the Earth hot inside. The heavy elements (including the radioactive U, Th, K) were forged in earlier stars and supernovae (fusion releases energy only up to iron; heavier elements need supernovae).

Evidence for the Big Bang (3): (1) the night sky is dark (Olbers’ paradox), so the universe is finite in extent and age; (2) galaxies show redshift (Hubble, Doppler effect), so they are moving apart and the universe is expanding; (3) the cosmic microwave background: the universe is 3 K, not 0 K, so residual heat from a hotter past remains.

Solar-system formation details worth knowing: the spinning cloud flattened into a disk (the plane of the ecliptic); planets formed fast (<100 Myr), first by electrostatic clumping and then by gravity; inner planets are small and rocky, outer planets are large and gaseous; an early strong solar wind cleared the leftover gas. Earth differentiated early (first tens of Myr): dense iron sank to form the core, leaving mantle (~80% of Earth’s volume) and crust on top.

Where the Sun’s 170,000 TW goes: ~30% reflected (albedo); ~50% absorbed as heat by land, sea and air (fuel for storms); ~20% evaporates water (evaporation takes an enormous amount of energy); ~0.02% photosynthesis. The Sun radiates mostly visible light (which is why our eyes evolved to see it); Earth radiates infrared.

How the sources interact: tides. The Moon’s gravity (and to a lesser extent the Sun’s) raises tidal bulges in the oceans and the solid Earth. As Earth rotates, rotational/orbital energy is transferred into moving ocean water and slightly heating the solid Earth. For Earth, tidal heating is negligible compared with primordial + radiogenic heat. For Io (Jupiter’s moon), tidal heating from Jupiter is so strong it powers Io’s volcanoes, so orbital energy becomes “interior” heat.

“Original energy source” questions (HW1 #4, class poll): trace the chain all the way back. - Oil/gas furnace, gas stove, gasoline → Sun (fossil fuels = buried organisms whose energy came from photosynthesis). The poll trick question: 76% said Earth’s interior; the answer is the Sun. Earth’s heat and pressure only transformed the material. - Ethanol bus, your run, wind, hydroelectric dam (evaporation → rain → river) → Sun. - Old Faithful geyser, Iceland geothermal → Earth’s interior. - Ocean tides → orbital/rotational energy (gravity of the Moon and Sun), not sunlight. - Nuclear power → uranium made in supernovae, mined from Earth (interior / stellar origin), not the Sun.

1.3 Physics toolkit (conceptual; no calculations on the midterm)

Quantity Meaning Unit (MKS)
Force push or pull; F = ma (Newton’s 2nd law) newton, N = kg·m/s² (an apple’s weight ≈ 1 N)
Work energy transferred by a force; W = force × distance moved joule, J = N·m = kg·m²/s²
Energy the capacity to do work (“anything that can be turned into heat”) joule (J)
Power rate of energy transfer watt, W = J/s (1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J)
Gravitational PE stored energy of position, U = mgh J
Kinetic energy energy of motion, KE = ½mv² (heat is molecular KE) J
Heat energy transferred between a system and its surroundings J (1 calorie = 4.184 J raises 1 g of water by 1 °C)
  • MKS units = meters, kilograms, seconds. Use them in calculations even though intuition often lives in mph or miles.
  • Energy conservation: energy is never created or destroyed; it converts among potential, kinetic and heat. Always ask “where did the energy go?” (The dropped apple’s KE went into deforming/bruising it plus a bit of heat.)
  • Sensible heat = heat that changes temperature (you can sense it).
  • Latent heat = heat that changes state (phase) without changing temperature. Melting ice absorbs latent heat at 0 °C; evaporating water absorbs latent heat; the reverse (freezing, condensation) releases it.
  • Heat capacity = how much heat is needed to raise an object’s temperature by 1 degree (a sensible-heat property). Water’s is very high.
  • Scientific notation a × 10ᵇ; precision: a result can’t be more precise than its least precise input (tsunami speed ≈ 230 m/s, not 232.282156 m/s).
  • Estimating Earth’s mass: assume Earth is a sphere of rock (density ≈ 3,300 kg/m³ from a hand-sized rock) → M ≈ ρ·(4/3)πR³ ≈ 3.6 × 10²⁴ kg vs. the true 6 × 10²⁴ kg. Why it’s too low: the core is iron (much denser than rock), and deep rocks are compressed by pressure.

1.4 Exam-style questions: Unit 1

Q: What are the three energy sources for natural disasters, and which kind of disaster does each power? Earth’s internal heat powers geological disasters (earthquakes, volcanoes). Solar energy powers meteorological disasters (hurricanes, storms, floods). Orbital energy (KE + gravitational PE of bodies orbiting the Sun) powers cosmological disasters (impacts). All three trace back to the Big Bang and the gravitational collapse that formed the solar system, which converted gravitational PE into KE and heat.

Q: Where does Earth’s internal heat come from? Two roughly equal sources (~50/50 of 46 TW). (1) Primordial heat: when Earth accreted, colliding bodies converted their orbital/gravitational energy into KE and then heat on impact; Earth is still losing that heat. (2) Radiogenic heat: radioactive decay of U, Th and K releases particles whose energy is absorbed as heat.

Q: Explain the difference between latent and sensible heat. Why does it matter for disasters? Sensible heat changes an object’s temperature. Latent heat changes its phase (solid ↔︎ liquid ↔︎ gas) at constant temperature. Water absorbs huge latent heat when it evaporates (taking up ~20–23% of solar input) and releases it when water vapor condenses in rising air. That released heat warms the air, makes it more buoyant, and powers storms and hurricanes.

Q: Explain why ocean tides are an example of energy sources interacting. Tides come from the gravitational pull of the Moon (and Sun) raising bulges in the oceans and solid Earth. As Earth rotates through these bulges, orbital/rotational energy is transferred into moving ocean water and a small amount of heat in the solid Earth. So orbital (cosmological-type) energy shows up inside Earth’s systems. On Io, the same tidal heating is strong enough to power volcanoes.

UNIT 2 · Our mobile planet (Lecture 4)

2.1 Official key points

  • Thermal convection gives us a mobile surface (plate tectonics) and a mobile atmosphere (atmospheric circulation). This mobility drives natural disasters.
  • Abundant water helps drive disasters because of its high heat capacity and high latent heat of freezing/condensation. Water “holds” a lot of heat.
  • Key aspects of Earth’s planetary evolution shape its disasters; other terrestrial planets look very different (e.g., no plate tectonics).
  • Earth’s position in its orbit and its rotation rate matter for driving disasters.

2.2 Thermal convection: “hot stuff rises, cold stuff falls”

  • Most materials expand and become less dense when heated and contract and become denser when cooled.
  • Material heated from below becomes buoyant and rises; material cooled at the top becomes heavy and sinks. Hot upwellings and cold downwellings avoid each other and set up a regular pattern of convection cells (Rayleigh–Bénard convection; pot of water on a stove; cloud streets).
  • Convection turns heat (energy) into motion. That is why we have a mobile, hazardous planet.

Two convecting systems:

Mantle (geological) Atmosphere (meteorological)
Heated by the core below plus internal radioactive heat (46 TW total; 38 TW comes out of the mantle, 8 TW is from radioactivity in the crust) the Sun (170,000 TW), heating the equator more than the poles
Material ~2,900 km of SOLID rock that flows very slowly (1–10 cm/yr, fingernail speed) air (and ocean water)
Result plate tectonics → earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain building; energy concentrated in upwellings and downwellings, i.e., at plate boundaries and hotspots heat carried from the equator to the poles → winds, storms, weather
Effect of rotation — Coriolis effect breaks one equator-to-pole cell into 3 cells per hemisphere (Hadley, Ferrel, Polar). Trade winds, westerlies and easterlies sit at their bases; jet streams at their tops.

2.3 Water is “super weird,” and that drives disasters

  • Water is a polar molecule with hydrogen bonds, which give it unusual energy properties:
    • very high heat capacity: it stores lots of sensible heat (oceans moderate climate and store energy);
    • very high latent heat of melting (3.8 × 10⁵ J/kg) and especially of vaporization/condensation (2.3 × 10⁶ J/kg);
    • ice is less dense than liquid water, so ice floats (icebergs, lake ice).
  • Water is a major energy carrier: evaporation takes up about 23% of solar power input. When that vapor condenses in rising air, it releases latent heat, reheating and boosting the upwelling. That is the engine of thunderstorms and hurricanes.
  • The hydrologic cycle uses solar heat to drive rivers, glaciers and erosion, and it drives the geological carbon cycle, which controls climate. “Water amplifies the effects of convection.”
  • Water also matters for plate tectonics (it weakens rock; it causes melting in subduction zones, Unit 6).

2.4 Why Earth is the most “mobile” terrestrial planet

Factor Earth Other planets

Orbit (distance from Sun) In the habitable zone, so liquid water exists. Water drives weather and helps plate tectonics, and life exists. Venus (too hot): runaway greenhouse. Water vapor is a greenhouse gas → hotter → more vapor; surface ≈ 470 °C; sunlight split H₂O and the H escaped → almost no water, no life, apparently no plate tectonics. Mars (too cold): water frozen in polar caps and permafrost; no plate tectonics today (maybe in the past; there is evidence of past flows and gullies).

Mars spins like Earth → some cyclonic structures and huge dust storms.

2.5 Exam-style questions: Unit 2

Q: How does convection give Earth both a mobile surface and a mobile atmosphere? In both the mantle and the atmosphere, material heated from below expands and becomes less dense, so it rises; material cooled at the top becomes denser and sinks. In the mantle, heat from the core and radioactive decay drives slow convection of solid rock, and the plates are the cold top boundary layer of that convection (plate tectonics). In the atmosphere, solar heating (strongest at the equator) drives air circulation that carries heat poleward. Both turn heat into motion, and that motion is what produces earthquakes, volcanoes and storms.

Q: Why does water make Earth’s disasters more energetic? Its high heat capacity lets the oceans store enormous heat, and its high latent heat means evaporation stores solar energy as water vapor (about 23% of incoming solar power). When the vapor condenses in rising air, that latent heat is released, warming and accelerating the updraft, which powers thunderstorms and hurricanes.

Q: Why does Earth have plate tectonics while Mars and the Moon don’t? Earth is big enough to have retained much of its internal heat, so its mantle is still hot enough to convect and break the lithosphere into moving plates. Small bodies like Mars, Mercury and the Moon lost their heat quickly, so their mantles convect weakly or not at all and they have a single stagnant lid. Liquid water on Earth (thanks to its orbit) also helps plate tectonics.

Q: Why is Earth’s rotation rate important for disasters? Fast rotation produces a strong Coriolis effect, which breaks the single equator-to-pole convection cell into three cells per hemisphere, creates jet streams, and makes storms rotate (cyclones, hurricanes). Rotation also drives core flow that generates the magnetic field. Slowly rotating Venus has no cyclonic weather.

UNIT 3 · Plate tectonics (Lectures 5–6)

3.1 Official key points

  • The mantle is solid rock but able to flow and convect. We know this from isostasy and post-glacial rebound. Plate tectonics is the surface manifestation of mantle convection.
  • The lithosphere (crust + top of the mantle; cold and therefore strong and resistant to flow) is broken into plates separated by plate boundaries.
  • The plate tectonic cycle: new oceanic plate is created at spreading centers and old plate is destroyed/recycled at subduction zones.
  • Three types of plate boundaries: divergent, convergent, transform.
  • Lines of evidence: shapes of continents; magnetic stripes; paleomagnetism; hotspot tracks; distribution of earthquakes and volcanoes; deep earthquakes; age, depth and sediment of ocean basins; satellite (GPS) measurements.
  • The three boundary types behave very differently for earthquakes and volcanoes: divergent = smallest earthquakes, convergent = largest, transform = in the middle.
  • Three kinds of convergent boundary: ocean–ocean, ocean–continent, continent–continent. Convergent boundaries are the most dangerous for earthquakes; subduction zones (ocean–ocean, ocean–continent) are the most dangerous of all.

3.2 How do we know a SOLID mantle can flow?

A rock on your stove will never convect, because at surface temperature and pressure rock is stiff. But at mantle temperatures and pressures, solid rock flows very slowly (like Silly Putty). Two observations prove it:

  1. Isostasy (proves the mantle can flow). Less-dense continents “float” (air quotes) in the denser mantle like wood blocks or icebergs in water. A floating body sinks until the weight of mantle it displaces equals its own weight. Taller blocks stick up higher and have deeper roots; a bigger density contrast makes them ride higher. Early-20th-century gravity surveys in the Himalayas showed the mountains have deep, low-density roots. Crust can only reach this floating balance if the mantle beneath can flow out of the way.
  2. Post-glacial rebound / glacial isostatic adjustment (tells us how easily it flows, i.e., its viscosity). During the last ice age (~20,000 years ago), 1–2 km of ice pushed Scandinavia and North America (even New Haven) down into the mantle. When the ice melted, the land began slowly rising back, like a memory-foam mattress after you lift your hand, and it is still rising today (measured by GPS). The rebound rate gives the mantle’s viscosity: ~10²⁰–10²¹ Pa·s, about 10²⁰ times more viscous than molasses.

3.3 Layers: compositional vs. mechanical

  • Compositional (chemical) layers: crust (continental: felsic, ~2,700 kg/m³; oceanic: mafic, ~7 km thick), mantle (~2,900 km of solid silicate rock, ~3,300 kg/m³), core (iron; liquid outer core makes the magnetic field, solid inner core).
  • Mechanical (strength) layers:
    • Lithosphere = crust + uppermost mantle. Cold → stiff/strong, behaves rigidly and breaks brittly. This is what the plates are.
    • Asthenosphere = warmer mantle below; solid but weak, flows relatively easily.
    • Deeper mantle: still solid and convecting, more viscous.
  • Why is the lithosphere strong? Temperature. Mantle rheology is temperature-dependent: cold rock is far more viscous (“slow as molasses in winter”). Plates are simply the cold top of the convecting system.

3.4 The plate tectonic cycle (“birth to death of an oceanic plate”)

  1. At a mid-ocean ridge / spreading center, two plates move apart. Mantle rises passively beneath to fill the gap (conservation of mass), partially melts by decompression, and the melt freezes into new oceanic crust.
  2. The new plate moves away from the ridge (seafloor spreading), carrying any continent “along for the ride.”
  3. As it ages it cools, thickens and becomes denser, so it sits lower (deeper ocean).
  4. When it is old, cold and dense enough, it sinks back into the mantle at a subduction zone under gravity.
  5. ⭐ The sinking slab pulls the rest of the plate behind it (slab pull). That pull is what drives plate motion and opens the ridge. Ridges are passive; nothing pushes up from below.
  6. One cycle takes ~200–250 Myr, so the oldest seafloor is only ~200 Myr old, while continental rocks (too buoyant to subduct) reach ~4 billion years.

Subduction is part of mantle convection, not separate from it: slabs are the cold downwellings, plumes are the hot upwellings.

3.6 The eight lines of evidence for plate tectonics (explain each!)

Evidence Observation Why it supports plate tectonics
1. Shapes of continents Coastlines across the Atlantic fit, best at the ~1,800 m depth contour (edge of the continental shelf = true boundary of continental crust) The continents were once joined (Pangaea) and have moved apart.
2. Magnetic stripes on the seafloor (“smoking gun,” Vine & Matthews) Bands of normal/reversed magnetized rock run parallel to ridges, are symmetric on both sides, and match the dated sequence of magnetic reversals New crust forms at the ridge, records the field as it cools, and moves away on both sides like two conveyor belts. Each reversal makes mirror-image stripes. This proves seafloor spreading.
3. Paleomagnetism Magnetic minerals in old rocks freeze in the field’s inclination (horizontal at the equator, vertical at the poles), so each rock records the latitude where it formed Rocks whose frozen field doesn’t match today’s field formed elsewhere. Dating them traces continents’ past positions (Pangaea ~220 Myr ago → Laurasia + Gondwana → today). Gives latitude, not longitude.
4. Hotspot tracks Hawaiian–Emperor chain: active volcano over the hotspot, islands get progressively older to the NW (Midway ~27 Myr, bend, Emperor seamounts ~65+ Myr) The hotspot is anchored in the deep mantle and the plate moves over it. The ages give the plate’s direction and speed.
5. Distribution of earthquakes & volcanoes They occur in narrow belts that outline plate boundaries (mostly, but not entirely: Hawaii, intraplate quakes) Plate boundaries are where plates interact.
6. Deep earthquakes (Wadati–Benioff zones) Earthquakes as deep as ~700 km, along dipping planes beneath trenches Normal hot mantle flows rather than breaking. Only cold, brittle subducting slabs can host deep quakes: direct evidence of subduction.
7. Age, depth and sediment of ocean basins Seafloor is youngest at ridges and older away from them (max ~200 Myr); ocean gets deeper with age; sediment is thin at ridges and thicker toward trenches Crust is born at ridges and moves away. It cools and becomes denser, sinking lower by isostasy, and it accumulates sediment over time. Ocean basins are young because they’re continuously recycled.
8. Satellite (GPS) measurements Precise GPS stations move a few cm/yr (1–10 cm/yr) Direct, present-day measurement of plate motion. Not available when the theory was developed.

History: Ortelius (1570) and Bacon (1620) noticed the fit. Suess named Gondwanaland. Wegener (1915) proposed continental drift and Pangaea, but was rejected because he had no mechanism: continents could not plow through stiff rock. Post-WWII ocean surveys (bathymetry: mid-ocean ridges, trenches over 11 km deep, fracture zones; magnetics) and Cold War seismic networks (built to detect nuclear tests) supplied the evidence. The fix: continents don’t plow through anything; they ride along inside plates, and the oceanic lithosphere is recycled. Accepted in the 1960s–70s; it rewrote the textbooks in about 5 years.

3.7 Why boundary type controls earthquake size (she said “all of that information is stuff I want you to retain”)

Earthquake size depends on how much elastic stress/strain can build up before the fault fails. That depends on the frictional contact area (fault area), how strong the rock is (cold and thick is stronger), and whether it’s in tension or compression (rocks are weak in tension, strong in compression). - Divergent: young lithosphere is hot and thin → small contact area; tension → rock fails easily before much stress builds → small earthquakes. (The exception is continental rifts, which can host larger ones.) - Transform: moderate temperature and thickness, modest contact area, shear → intermediate (can still be deadly: Haiti 2010 M7.0, Turkey 2023 M7.8). - Convergent / subduction: old, cold, thick plates; a huge, gently dipping contact (megathrust) hundreds to >1,000 km long; compression (rock is strong) → enormous stored energy → largest earthquakes (M9+). The Pacific “Ring of Fire” has most of them.

3.8 Exam-style questions: Unit 3

Q: The mantle is solid. How do we know it can flow, and how do we know how easily it flows? Isostasy: continents and mountains “float” in the denser mantle with deep low-density roots (shown by Himalayan gravity surveys). That balance is only possible if the mantle can flow out of the way, so the mantle can flow. Post-glacial rebound: land that was pressed down by kilometer-thick ice sheets is still slowly rising ~20,000 years after the ice melted (Scandinavia, Canada), like memory foam. The rate of that rebound tells us the mantle’s viscosity, i.e., how easily it flows (very slowly, ~10²¹ Pa·s).

Q: What drives plate motion? Mantle convection. Specifically, old oceanic lithosphere cools and becomes denser than the mantle beneath it, so it sinks at subduction zones under gravity and pulls the rest of the plate behind it (slab pull). At ridges, the mantle rises passively to fill the gap. Plates are the cold upper boundary layer of the convecting mantle, so plate tectonics is the surface manifestation of mantle convection.

Q: Why is the seafloor never older than ~200 Myr when continents are ~4 Byr old? Oceanic lithosphere is created at ridges and, once it is old, cold and dense enough, it sinks back into the mantle at subduction zones. It is constantly recycled on a ~200–250 Myr cycle. Continental crust is felsic and low-density (too buoyant to subduct), so it stays at the surface and can be billions of years old.

Q: Explain how magnetic stripes on the seafloor support seafloor spreading. Magma erupts at the ridge; as it cools, magnetic minerals lock in the direction of Earth’s magnetic field. The field reverses every ~10,000–100,000 years. As the new crust is carried away on both sides, each reversal leaves a pair of stripes. The stripes are parallel to the ridge, symmetric on either side, and match the dated reversal timescale. That pattern only makes sense if new seafloor is continuously made at the ridge and spreads outward.

Q: Why do subduction zones have the biggest earthquakes and spreading centers the smallest? At spreading centers the lithosphere is hot and thin (small frictional contact area) and in tension (rock is weak in tension), so faults slip before much stress accumulates. At subduction zones the plates are cold and thick, the fault contact is enormous, and the rocks are in compression (strong), so huge amounts of elastic energy can build up and be released at once, producing M9 earthquakes. Transforms are in between.

Q: Why does the ocean get deeper away from mid-ocean ridges? The lithosphere is hot and thermally expanded at the ridge. As it moves away it cools, contracts and becomes denser, so by isostasy it sinks lower into the mantle. Older seafloor is therefore deeper.

Q: What are the three types of convergent boundaries, and what happens at each? Ocean–ocean: the older, denser plate subducts → island arc (Marianas, Aleutians, Japan). Ocean–continent: the oceanic plate subducts beneath the buoyant continent → continental arc (Andes, Cascades). Continent–continent: neither subducts → collision, crustal thickening, mountains (Himalaya), large earthquakes but no subduction volcanism.

4.1 Official key points

  • Convection: what it is and why it happens (lava lamp analogy).
  • Mantle rheology: viscous, brittle, elastic deformation (Silly Putty analogy).
  • Factors that affect rheology: strain rate (how fast it’s deformed) and temperature (temperature-dependent viscosity).
  • Isostasy: less-dense continents “floating” on a higher-density mantle.
  • Example isostasy problem: what percentage of an iceberg is below the surface, controlled by the density of ice vs. water.

4.2 Viscosity

  • Viscosity = a fluid’s resistance to flow (“stickiness,” “thickness”). It is a material property.
  • Units: pascal-seconds (Pa·s) = N·s/m². (From the two-plate experiment, F = μ·A·u/y → μ = (F/A)·(y/u): force per area × time.)
  • Typical values: air 2×10⁻⁵ · water 10⁻³ · motor oil 0.3 · corn syrup 1 · molasses 5 · peanut butter ~250 (poll) · Silly Putty ~10⁵ · mantle ~10²¹ Pa·s.
  • “Fluid” ≠ “liquid.” The mantle is a fluid (it flows) but a solid (not a liquid).

Why a rock sinks more slowly in syrup than in water (corn-syrup demo): two forces compete. Gravity pulls the denser rock down (net of buoyancy). The viscous drag of the surrounding fluid resists the motion, and that drag force is proportional to the fluid’s viscosity (and the rock’s speed). The rock quickly reaches a terminal velocity where drag balances gravity. Higher viscosity means drag balances gravity at a much lower speed. The rock still sinks eventually (it’s denser), just slowly, like a rock in peanut butter or a cold slab in the mantle (~10 cm/yr, HW2).

4.3 Convection: what it is and why it happens

  • What: heat transfer by bulk motion of material: hot, less-dense material rises; cold, denser material sinks; a circulating cell forms.
  • Lava lamp: the bulb heats wax at the bottom → it expands, becomes less dense, rises → cools at the top → becomes denser, sinks → repeats.
    • Limitations of the analogy: (1) the lava lamp has two different materials (wax and liquid), while the mantle’s up- and downwellings are mostly the same material at different temperatures; (2) a lava lamp has no plates; its top doesn’t participate, whereas Earth’s cold top layer (the lithosphere) is part of the convection.
  • Whether something convects depends on: the temperature difference top-to-bottom (bigger → more likely), the size of the system (bigger → more likely), the material’s thermal properties, and viscosity (higher → less likely).
  • Why does the mantle convect despite a viscosity of ~10²¹ Pa·s? Because the temperature difference is huge (thousands of °C at the core–mantle boundary vs. ~10 °C at the surface) and the mantle is enormous (2,900 km thick). Those factors overwhelm the high viscosity.

4.4 Rheology: how materials deform (Silly Putty day)

Deformation What happens Silly Putty In the Earth
Viscous flows, permanent, doesn’t spring back pull slowly → it stretches like taffy mantle convection (and post-glacial rebound)
Brittle breaks / fractures yank fast → it snaps the cold lithosphere breaking in earthquakes (faults)
Elastic deforms and springs back to its original shape (stores, then returns, energy) roll a ball and bounce it seismic waves are elastic waves; rocks near faults bend elastically before an earthquake (elastic rebound)

What controls which behavior you get? 1. Strain rate (how fast you deform it): slow → viscous; fast → brittle; very fast/short-lived → elastic. The same mantle is viscous over millions of years (convection) but elastic over seconds (seismic waves pass through it). 2. Temperature (the more important control in the Earth): hot → low viscosity, flows; cold → stiff, strong, brittle (“slow as molasses in winter”). Temperature-dependent rheology is why we have plates: the cold top of the mantle (lithosphere) is too stiff to flow and breaks in earthquakes, while the hot mantle below flows.

4.5 Isostasy and the iceberg (THE example she said could be on the exam)

  • Isostasy: a less-dense block floats in a denser fluid, sinking until the weight of displaced fluid equals the block’s weight (Archimedes). Continental crust (~2,700 kg/m³) “floats” on mantle (~3,300 kg/m³). Tall mountains have deep roots (like an iceberg’s hidden bulk); a 4-km-high range has a root ~18 km deep (HW2).

Conceptual iceberg explanation (no formulas needed): > An iceberg floats because ice (~920 kg/m³) is slightly less dense than seawater (~1,000 kg/m³). The berg isn’t moving, so the forces on it must balance: the downward pull of gravity on the whole iceberg must equal the upward buoyant force, which equals the weight of the water the submerged part pushes aside. Because ice is only a little less dense than water, the berg has to push aside almost its own volume of water to make up its weight, so most of it (~90%, more precisely 920/1000 ≈ 92%) sits below the waterline and only ~10% sticks up. The fraction submerged equals the ratio of densities (ρ_ice/ρ_water). A material much less dense than water (a beach ball, cork, styrofoam) floats mostly above the surface. Continents work the same way: crust is ~82% the density of mantle, so mountains have roots several times deeper than their height.

  • Class method: F₁ (weight of the part above water, pulling down) + F₂ (net upward buoyancy of the submerged part, which acts like a “negative mass” because it is less dense than the water it replaced, like a beach ball pushed under water) = 0, because the berg isn’t accelerating (F = ma with a = 0).
  • Applications of isostasy across the course: continents stand high because felsic crust is less dense; mountains have roots; ocean floor deepens as it cools and densifies; hotspots make islands because the extra melt builds thick crust that pokes above sea level; post-glacial rebound.

4.6 Exam-style questions: Unit 4

Q: Define viscosity and give its units. Why does a rock fall more slowly through corn syrup than water? Viscosity is a fluid’s resistance to flow, measured in Pa·s. A falling rock feels gravity (pulling it down because it’s denser than the fluid) and viscous drag (resisting its motion), and drag grows with viscosity. It quickly reaches terminal velocity where the two balance; in a more viscous fluid that balance occurs at a much lower speed.

Q: Compare the lava lamp to mantle convection. How is it similar, and where does the analogy break down? Similar: heat at the bottom makes material expand, become less dense and rise; it cools at the top, becomes denser and sinks, forming convection. Different: the lava lamp uses two different materials, while the mantle’s rising and sinking rock is mostly the same material at different temperatures. And the lamp has no plates, while Earth’s cold top boundary layer (the lithosphere) breaks into plates that take part in the convection.

Q: Use Silly Putty to explain the three ways the mantle deforms. What controls which one happens? Pulled slowly, Silly Putty flows (viscous), like the convecting mantle over millions of years. Yanked quickly, it snaps (brittle), like the cold lithosphere breaking in earthquakes. Bounced, it springs back (elastic), like rocks transmitting seismic waves. Strain rate controls the behavior (slow → viscous, fast → brittle/elastic). In the Earth, temperature matters even more: cold rock is stiff and breaks, hot rock flows. That’s why the cold lithosphere forms rigid plates over the flowing mantle.

Q: Explain conceptually why ~90% of an iceberg is below the water. (See the boxed answer in §4.5.) Ice is only slightly less dense than water, so to be supported the iceberg must displace a weight of water equal to its own weight; that requires submerging ~90% of its volume. Fraction submerged = ρ_ice/ρ_water.

UNIT 5 · Earthquakes (Lectures 8–11)

5.1 Official key points

  • Earthquakes occur mainly on plate boundaries; different boundaries → different types of earthquakes.
  • Earthquakes happen when blocks of rock slip past each other across a fault; that generates elastic waves.
  • Fault types: normal, reverse (thrust), strike-slip.
  • Earthquake properties: location (hypocenter/epicenter) and magnitude.
  • Elastic rebound theory: stress builds up on faults from plate motion; the fault eventually ruptures; the elastic “rebound” generates seismic waves.
  • Wave types: body waves (P, S) and surface waves (Love, Rayleigh); wave properties amplitude, wavelength, frequency, period.
  • Seismograms record ground motion in 3 directions (vertical + two horizontal); you can see each wave type on them.
  • Locating earthquakes with the S–P time delay at multiple stations.
  • Magnitude scales (Richter, moment magnitude) are all logarithmic: +1 = 10× the shaking.
  • Different waves do different damage; Love waves are the most damaging (horizontal motion; buildings are designed for vertical loads). Wave frequency matters. Site amplification matters.
  • Boundary → fault type: convergent = reverse/thrust, divergent = normal, transform = strike-slip.
  • Famous examples (concepts, not dates): normal L’Aquila 2009; strike-slip Haiti 2010; thrust/subduction Chile 1960, Alaska 1964, Sumatra 2004, Japan 2011 (all M9+); western US/Mexico (Cascadia, Mexico City, Loma Prieta, San Andreas).
  • Stick-slip behavior and seismic gaps, and how the slider-block demo illustrates them.
  • Seismic waves are elastic waves (Slinky demo). What each wave’s motion looks like.
  • A simple “earthquake location” problem, done conceptually.

5.2 What is an earthquake?

  • Earthquake = shaking of Earth’s rocks as elastic waves pass through them. Most commonly it is caused by sudden movement of rock across a fault. (Seismic waves can also come from volcanoes, landslides, impacts and nuclear tests; Cold War seismic networks were built to detect bomb tests.)
  • Fault = a fracture in the Earth across which the two sides move relative to each other.
  • Cause: plate motion builds stress until it exceeds the strength of the rock (friction on the fault); then the rock breaks and slips.
  • Hypocenter (focus) = the point on the fault where rupture starts, including its depth. Epicenter = the point on the surface directly above the hypocenter.
  • An earthquake is not a point: rupture starts at the hypocenter and propagates across a large fault area. Bigger earthquakes rupture bigger areas (Sumatra 2004: ~1,200 km of fault over ~7–8 minutes).
  • Foreshocks (before), mainshock (largest), aftershocks (after). Nearly all big quakes have aftershocks. ❓ Foreshocks can’t be used for prediction because you only know a quake was a foreshock after a bigger one happens.

5.3 Fault types (know the stress → fault → boundary chain)

Dip-slip terms: hanging wall = block above the fault plane; footwall = block below (stand in a mine on the fault: your feet are on the footwall).

Fault Motion Stress Typical boundary Can also occur Example
Normal hanging wall moves down relative to footwall extension (pulling apart) divergent (ridges, rifts) pull-apart bends in strike-slip faults (Dead Sea); bending overriding plate above subduction zones (Italy) L’Aquila 2009; Teton fault
Reverse / thrust hanging wall moves up relative to footwall compression (pushing together) convergent (subduction, collision) push-together bends on strike-slip faults (Transverse Ranges, CA) Chile 1960, Alaska 1964, Sumatra 2004, Tohoku 2011

5.4 Elastic rebound and stick-slip (the earthquake cycle)

  • Elastic rebound theory: the plates move steadily, but the fault is locked by friction. The rocks on either side bend elastically, storing strain energy (fences, roads and walls across the fault bend). When stress exceeds the fault’s frictional strength, it ruptures, and the rocks snap back (“rebound”) to an unstrained shape in a new, offset position (the 1992 Landers quake offset the highway lines). The sudden rebound releases the stored elastic energy as seismic waves.
  • Stick-slip: instead of sliding smoothly, a high-friction fault sticks (stress builds), then slips suddenly (earthquake), then sticks again. The cycle repeats. (Some faults with low friction creep steadily instead, like the central creeping section of the San Andreas, with many small quakes and no big ones.)

Slider-block demo (she wants you to EXPLAIN it): - Setup: a brick (or block) on sandpaper (the fault with high friction), pulled by a bungee cord / rubber band (the elastic crust), whose far end is pulled steadily by her hand (plate motion). - What happens: the hand moves steadily, but the brick stays put while the cord stretches and stores elastic energy (“stick”). When the cord’s pull exceeds friction, the brick lurches forward (“slip”) = the earthquake. The cord relaxes; stress builds again; repeat. Steady plate motion produces episodic earthquakes. It is like pushing an old fridge until it suddenly lurches. - Two-brick version = seismic gaps: brick 1 (pulled directly) slips several times; each slip stretches the cord to brick 2, transferring stress to it. Brick 2 stays locked until enough stress has accumulated, then slips (often triggered by a brick-1 slip, and sometimes both lurch together in a bigger event). The locked brick 2 is the seismic gap: a segment that hasn’t slipped but is being loaded and is “ready to go.”

Seismic gap = a segment of an active fault that hasn’t ruptured in a long time while its neighbors have. It is still being loaded by plate motion and by stress transferred from neighboring ruptures, so it is the most likely place for the next big earthquake. It tells you where, not when. - Examples: Shumagin Gap (Alaska–Aleutians); the North Anatolian Fault ruptured segment by segment westward from 1939 to 1999 (Izmit), leaving a gap next to Istanbul; the Michoacán gap filled by the 1985 Mexico earthquake; Cascadia (locked since 1700); locked San Andreas segments north of LA and in the Bay Area.

5.5 Seismic waves (know the motions COLD)

All seismic waves are elastic waves: rock is compressed/sheared, stores elastic potential energy, springs back, and pushes the next bit of rock. The wave moves by continual exchange between elastic PE and KE (Slinky; human-chain demo).

Wave Family Particle motion vs. direction of travel Speed / order Damage & notes
P (primary) body parallel: push-pull compression and expansion (longitudinal), like sound or a Slinky pushed along its length fastest, arrives first (~5 km/s in crust, ~8 km/s in upper mantle) travels through solids, liquids and gases; small amplitude, high frequency; on a distant seismogram, mostly vertical
S (secondary) body perpendicular: side-to-side or up-down shearing (transverse), like a shaken rope second (~3 km/s); P is ~1.7–2× faster cannot travel through liquids (no rigidity, so nothing pulls the next particle back); larger than P
Love surface horizontal, side to side perpendicular to travel (like a slithering snake) slower than body waves ⭐ most damaging: pure horizontal shaking
Rayleigh surface rolling elliptical motion (up-down and forward-back, retrograde), like ocean waves; dies off with depth slowest-ish, arrives last mostly vertical motion, so less damaging than Love
  • Body waves travel through the Earth’s interior: faster, higher frequency, smaller amplitude.
  • Surface waves are trapped near the surface: slower, lower frequency (longer period), larger amplitude, more damaging. Shallow earthquakes make strong surface waves; deep ones don’t (one reason deep quakes are less destructive).
  • Wave vocabulary: amplitude = size of displacement; wavelength = distance between crests; period = time between crests; frequency = waves per second = 1/period.

On a seismogram (3 components: vertical, N–S, E–W): first the small, high-frequency P wave (mostly vertical for distant quakes, because waves come up steeply beneath the station); then the larger S wave (mostly horizontal); then the big, long-period surface waves: Love (horizontal, perpendicular to the path) then Rayleigh (vertical + horizontal along the path). The shaking can continue for hours as waves circle the globe.

How a seismometer works: a mass on a spring inside a frame bolted to the ground. When the ground shakes, the frame moves but the mass’s inertia keeps it nearly still; the relative motion is recorded. Three masses record three directions. (The first device was a Chinese seismoscope, ~132 CE: dragons drop balls into frogs’ mouths.)

5.6 Locating an earthquake (conceptual: no algebra on the exam)

  1. P waves travel faster than S waves, so the farther a station is from the quake, the longer the S–P delay. (Think of two cars leaving the same city at different speeds; the gap between them grows with distance.)
  2. Measure the S–P time on a station’s seismogram → convert it to distance using known wave speeds (travel-time curves).
  3. The quake lies somewhere on a circle of that radius around the station.
  4. Two stations → two circles → two possible points (ambiguous).
  5. Three stations → three circles intersect at ONE point = the epicenter. This is trilateration (like triangulation; like old cell-phone tower location).
  • Lightning and thunder analogy: light arrives essentially instantly; sound travels ~0.2 mi/s, so count seconds between flash and thunder: ~5 s ≈ 1 mile. Three people counting in different places can pinpoint the strike. Earthquakes are the same, except P is only ~2× faster than S (instead of light being millions of times faster than sound).
  • Modern practice: computers find the location and origin time that best fit P and S arrivals at many stations of a global network. That’s how global earthquake maps (which outline plate boundaries) are made.

5.7 Size: magnitude vs. intensity

  • Magnitude = the size of the earthquake (energy released). One value per earthquake.
    • Richter (the first scale): log₁₀ of the peak wave amplitude on a seismogram, corrected for distance (as if 100 km away). Seismologists rarely use it now; it underestimates large or distant quakes.
    • Moment magnitude (Mw) (modern, most accurate): based on seismic moment = rock strength (rigidity) × fault rupture area × slip. Bigger fault + more slip = bigger quake. “Large earthquakes mean large faults.”
    • All scales are logarithmic: +1 magnitude = 10× the wave amplitude (shaking) and ~32× the energy. M9 shakes 100× more than M7 and releases ~1,000× the energy.
    • Rupture length: M4 ≈ 100 m, M5 ≈ 1 km, M6 ≈ 10 km, M7 ≈ 100 km, M8+ ≈ 1,000+ km. Largest recorded: M9.5 (Chile 1960). ❓ There is no M10: no fault on Earth is big enough.
  • Mercalli intensity = how strongly the ground shook at a particular place (I = not felt … XII = total destruction). It is qualitative (“felt” index) and varies from place to place for the same quake. It depends on: magnitude, distance from the hypocenter, duration of shaking, and local geology (soft sediment amplifies). Example: the M5.8 Virginia 2011 quake was felt over a far wider area than the M6.0 California 2004 quake, because the old, cold rocks of eastern North America transmit seismic energy very efficiently.

5.8 Why earthquakes damage buildings (earthquake engineering)

  1. Horizontal vs. vertical motion: buildings are designed to carry vertical loads (their own weight under gravity), so vertical shaking is relatively safe. Horizontal shaking pushes the foundation sideways while the building’s mass lags behind (inertia), shearing columns. Love waves (all horizontal) do the most damage; Rayleigh waves (mostly vertical) do less. “Soft first story” buildings (open ground floors on columns) are especially vulnerable.
  2. Resonance / frequency: every structure sways at a natural period (1-story house ≈ 0.1 s; 30-story tower ≈ 3 s; flexible wood/steel → longer periods; stiff brick/concrete → shorter). If the wave period matches the building’s period, shaking is amplified (resonance), a common cause of collapse. Surface waves are low-frequency/long-period, so they tend to match tall buildings and bridges.
  3. Rupture size → frequency → where damage happens: short, small ruptures make mostly high-frequency waves, which do damage near the epicenter but die out quickly with distance. Long, big ruptures make lots of low-frequency waves, which travel far and resonate with tall structures far away.
  4. Depth: shallow earthquakes generate strong surface waves; deep earthquakes don’t, so they are usually much less damaging (deep Colombia 2026 M7.4 at 110 km vs. shallow Venezuela doublet).
  5. Site amplification: soft sediments, mud, lake beds and artificial fill amplify shaking. Waves slow down in soft material, and to conserve energy their amplitude grows; the basin can also resonate (“shakes like a bowl of Jell-O”). Bedrock sites shake much less. Examples: Mexico City 1985 (old lake bed, 350–400 km from the epicenter, ~8,000 dead); Loma Prieta 1989 (the Nimitz Freeway/Cypress viaduct built on mud pancaked, 42 dead, and the structure resonated with the mud’s shaking); San Francisco 1906 (worst damage on fill); Boston’s Back Bay (fill).
  6. Human factors: building codes and construction quality (Haiti vs. Chile), warning systems, holidays, and communication of hazard (L’Aquila, Fukushima).

5.10 Exam-style questions: Unit 5

Q: What is an earthquake, and why does it generate seismic waves? (elastic rebound) An earthquake is sudden slip of rock across a fault. Plate motion slowly builds stress across a locked fault, and the rocks on either side bend elastically and store strain energy. When the stress exceeds the frictional strength of the fault, it ruptures and the rocks snap back (rebound) to an unstrained shape in an offset position. That sudden release of stored elastic energy sends out elastic (seismic) waves.

Q: Explain how the slider-block demonstration illustrates stick-slip behavior and seismic gaps. The hand moving steadily is plate motion; the bungee cord is the elastic crust; the brick on sandpaper is the fault held by friction. The brick stays stuck while the cord stretches and stores energy, then suddenly lurches when the pull exceeds friction (an earthquake). Then it sticks again, so steady motion produces episodic quakes (stick-slip, the earthquake cycle). With two bricks, the first slips repeatedly and each slip loads the cord to the second, which stays locked until enough stress accumulates. That locked, loaded segment is a seismic gap: it hasn’t moved, but stress is building and it is likely to produce the next large earthquake.

Q: Which fault type do you expect at a divergent boundary, and how big will the earthquakes be? Why? Normal faults, because the plates are pulling apart (extension), so the hanging wall drops down. The earthquakes will be small, because the lithosphere there is hot and thin (small fault area) and in tension, which rocks can’t sustain much of.

Q: Describe the four seismic wave types and which arrives first. P waves: compressional, particles move back and forth parallel to the wave’s travel; fastest, arrive first; travel through solids and liquids. S waves: shear, particles move perpendicular to travel; second; can’t pass through liquids. Love waves: surface waves with horizontal side-to-side shaking. Rayleigh waves: surface waves with a rolling, elliptical up-and-down motion like ocean swells. Surface waves arrive last but have the largest amplitudes.

Q: Why can’t S waves travel through a liquid? S waves move by shearing: each particle is displaced sideways, and the material’s rigidity (shear stiffness) pulls it back and drags the next particle along. Liquids have no rigidity (they don’t resist shear), so there’s no restoring force and the wave can’t propagate. (Human-chain demo: arms dropped = liquid, so nothing propagated.)

Q: Why are Love waves more damaging than Rayleigh waves, and surface waves more damaging than body waves? Surface waves have larger amplitudes (their energy stays near the surface) and lower frequencies that match the natural sway periods of buildings and bridges, causing resonance; they also travel far. Love waves are entirely horizontal, and buildings are designed to support vertical (gravity) loads, not sideways shaking, so horizontal motion shears and topples them. Rayleigh waves are mostly vertical.

Q: Explain how seismologists locate an earthquake. Because P waves travel faster than S waves, the time gap between their arrivals grows with distance (like the delay between lightning and thunder). The S–P time at a station gives the distance to the quake, which defines a circle around that station. Two stations give two possible points; a third circle picks the single intersection: the epicenter (trilateration).

Q: What is the difference between magnitude and intensity? Magnitude measures the size/energy of the earthquake itself; there is one number per quake, logarithmic (+1 = 10× amplitude, ~32× energy). Intensity (Mercalli) measures how strongly the ground shook at a particular location, so it varies from place to place. It depends on magnitude, distance, duration, and local geology.

Q: What is site amplification? Give an example. Soft sediments, mud, fill or old lake beds shake much more strongly than bedrock during the same earthquake: waves slow down in soft material, so their amplitude grows (energy conservation), and basins can resonate. Mexico City (1985) was 350+ km from the epicenter but suffered huge damage because it sits on an old lake bed; the Nimitz Freeway (1989) collapsed where it was built on mud.

Q: Why was the 2010 Haiti earthquake so much deadlier than larger earthquakes in Chile? Magnitude alone doesn’t determine damage. Haiti’s M7.0 was shallow and directly beneath densely populated Port-au-Prince, and buildings were poorly constructed with weak or unenforced codes. Chile’s larger quakes struck a country with strict, well-enforced building codes. Depth, proximity, construction and preparedness matter as much as size.

Q: What is a seismic gap, and why do scientists care? A segment of an active fault (often a subduction zone) that hasn’t ruptured in a long time while neighboring segments have. It keeps being loaded by plate motion and by stress transferred from neighboring ruptures, so it’s a likely site for the next large earthquake (e.g., the segment near Istanbul on the North Anatolian Fault, Cascadia). It tells us where to prepare, though not when.

6.1 Official key points

  • The mantle is solid (!!!) and convects to lose heat; heat transfer is focused into downwellings (slabs) and upwellings (plumes).
  • Almost all volcanism occurs in three settings: divergent boundaries (mid-ocean ridges), convergent boundaries (subduction zones), and hotspots (mantle plumes).
  • Divergent: underwater, not explosive, pillow basalt lava.
  • Hotspots: more melting and magma production, which is why there are ocean islands above them (Hawaii).
  • Subduction zones: the most explosive and dangerous eruptions.
  • Melting mechanisms: ridges and hotspots → decompression (pressure-release) melting; subduction zones → dehydration melting.
  • Volcanoes are scientifically valuable: they bring up mantle material. Ridge volcanoes sample the shallow mantle; hotspot volcanoes sample the deep mantle.
  • Most common crustal elements: O and Si → silicate tetrahedra.
  • Felsic (more silica) vs. mafic (less silica) rocks and magmas.
  • Bowen’s reaction series: on cooling, mafic minerals crystallize first (highest T) and felsic last; on heating, felsic minerals melt first (lowest T) and mafic last.
  • Rock types: sedimentary, metamorphic, igneous.
  • Magmas’ two key properties: viscosity and volatile content (dissolved H₂O, CO₂).
  • Viscosity is controlled by temperature, silica content, crystal content. Mafic = low viscosity, felsic = high.
  • Volatiles escape mafic magmas easily; felsic magmas trap them.
  • Felsic: higher viscosity + more volatiles → more dangerous eruptions. Mafic: lower viscosity + fewer volatiles → less dangerous.
  • Mafic magmas at ridges and hotspots; felsic magmas at subduction zones. Subducting slabs carry water and CO₂, so subduction magmas are volatile-rich.
  • Eruption styles: non-explosive (fire fountains, basalt flows, Strombolian) vs. explosive (Vulcanian, Plinian, caldera).
  • Ground flows: mafic lava flows (pahoehoe, a’a) vs. pyroclastic flows and lahars (felsic volcanoes).
  • Volcano types: shield (mafic; Hawaii) vs. stratovolcano = composite (felsic; Mt. St. Helens, Mt. Fuji).
  • Construction and shape are controlled by the three V’s: viscosity, volatiles, volume.

6.3 Why does solid mantle melt? (“I guarantee it will make it onto a midterm or a final”)

A hotter plume crosses the solidus deeper and makes more melt → islands.
  • Dehydration (flux, wet) melting (subduction zones): water released from the slab enters the hot mantle wedge. Water weakens silicate bonds and lowers the melting temperature, shifting the solidus to the left. The wedge’s temperature didn’t change, but now it sits on the liquid side of the wet solidus, so it melts. Analogy: salt on an icy sidewalk melts the ice without heating it.

6.4 Magma chemistry: the felsic–mafic master distinction

  • Magma = molten rock below the surface; lava = the same melt once erupted.
  • O and Si are the two most abundant crustal elements (8 elements make ~98% of the crust). Si needs 4 electrons and bonds to 4 oxygens, forming the SiO₄ silicate tetrahedron (tetrahedral to keep the electron pairs as far apart as possible). Each corner oxygen still wants one more bond, so tetrahedra link to each other at their corners, forming chains and sheets (like polymers, bread dough or egg whites). More silica → more linking → stickier melt. Tetrahedra also bond with metals (Mg, Fe, K…) to make minerals (olivine = Mg/Fe + SiO₄; quartz = pure SiO₂).
  • Mineral = naturally occurring, inorganic, crystalline solid. Rock = aggregate of one or more minerals.

6.5 The two magma properties that control everything

Viscosity: three controls, all making felsic sticky and mafic runny 1. Temperature: hotter → bonds break more easily, molecules more mobile → lower viscosity (“molasses in winter”). Mafic magmas are hotter (Bowen: mafic material only melts at high T); felsic magmas can stay molten at lower T, so they’re cooler and more viscous. 2. Silica content: SiO₄ tetrahedra link into long chains and sheets that tangle (bread dough, egg whites, polymers) → higher viscosity. Felsic = high silica = very viscous; mafic = low silica = runny. 3. Crystal content: more solid crystals → thicker slurry → higher viscosity. Cooler felsic magmas contain mafic components that have already crystallized (Bowen), so they carry more crystals; hot mafic magma has few.

Volatiles (dissolved H₂O and CO₂) - Solubility depends on pressure: deep in the chamber (high pressure), magma holds lots of dissolved gas. As magma rises, pressure drops and the gas comes out of solution as bubbles, like opening a shaken soda bottle. - Where they come from: mainly subduction zones: the slab carries water and CO₂ down. Ridges and hotspots have no comparable source. - Viscosity decides what the bubbles do: in runny mafic magma, bubbles rise and escape gently (no pressure buildup). In sticky felsic magma, bubbles are trapped, gas pressure builds until the magma fragments explosively. - ⭐ Explosiveness = high viscosity × high volatiles. Felsic + volatile-rich (subduction) = violent. Mafic + volatile-poor (ridge/hotspot) = gentle.

The central causal chain (why subduction volcanoes are the dangerous ones): > Slab carries H₂O + CO₂ down → water released into the mantle wedge lowers its melting point → cool, wet magma forms (initially mafic) → it rises through felsic continental crust and, because felsic minerals melt first (Bowen), melts and absorbs felsic material → the magma becomes felsic, silica-rich, highly viscous, and volatile-rich → gases are trapped → as it rises, pressure drops and gas exsolves → pressure builds → explosive eruption (Vulcanian → Plinian → caldera), pyroclastic flows, lahars, steep stratovolcanoes. (Island arcs pass through less crust, so they’re somewhat less felsic than continental arcs, but they’re still wet and explosive.) > > Contrast: ridges and hotspots → dry decompression melting of mantle → hot, mafic, low-viscosity, volatile-poor magma → gas escapes → gentle lava flows, shield volcanoes (or pillow basalts underwater).

6.7 Ground flows

  • Mafic lava flows (hotspots/ridges): pahoehoe (smooth, ropy, runny), a’a (thicker, blocky, rubbly, slightly more viscous), lava tubes (flowing under a cooled crust), lava lakes, pillow basalts (underwater), columnar/flood basalts (East Rock, Columbia River). They destroy property but rarely kill; you can walk away (Kalapana, Hawaii 1990).
  • Pyroclastic flows (nuée ardente, “glowing cloud”), from felsic explosive volcanoes: a ground-hugging avalanche of hot gas, ash and rock formed when an eruption column collapses under gravity or by a directed blast. They travel ~300 km/h (can’t outrun or out-drive them) at hundreds of °C and kill everything in their path. Mt. Pelée 1902 killed ~30,000 in ~10 seconds (2 survivors).
  • Lahars: volcanic ash and debris mixed with water (rain, rivers, melting snow and glaciers) → a fast mudflow with the consistency of wet cement (~50 km/h) that follows valleys. Nevado del Ruiz 1985 lahars killed >20,000.
  • ⭐ Pyroclastic flows and lahars are “the two big culprits” for volcanic fatalities, and both belong to felsic, subduction-zone volcanoes.

6.9 Volcanoes as “messengers from the mantle”

We can’t drill into the mantle (~80% of Earth’s volume), so we study it with seismic waves and with lavas that volcanoes bring up. Both are basalts, but their trace-element chemistry differs, which was a first clue that the deep and shallow mantle differ and that they don’t fully mix.

6.11 Exam-style questions: Unit 6

Q: Most of the mantle is solid. Explain why melting occurs beneath mid-ocean ridges and hotspots. The melting temperature of mantle rock increases with pressure: at high pressure it’s harder to break bonds and form liquid. Beneath ridges (plates pulled apart) and hotspots (buoyant plumes), hot solid mantle rises. It barely cools, but the pressure on it drops a lot, so its melting temperature falls below its actual temperature and it partially melts with no heat added. This is decompression (pressure-release) melting. Plumes are hotter, so they start melting deeper and produce more magma, building islands.

Q: Subduction zones are colder than the surrounding mantle. Why do they have volcanoes? The subducting slab carries water (in hydrated minerals) and CO₂ down. As it heats up at depth, the water is driven out into the overlying hot mantle wedge. Water weakens silicate bonds and lowers the wedge’s melting temperature, so the wedge melts even though its temperature hasn’t increased (dehydration/flux melting, like salt melting ice). The melt rises to feed an arc of volcanoes.

Q: What is the difference between felsic and mafic magma? Felsic magma is silica-rich (>65% SiO₂), richer in light elements, lower density, lower melting temperature, cooler, highly viscous, and good at trapping volatiles. It is typical of continental crust and subduction zones. Mafic magma is silica-poor and rich in Mg and Fe, denser, hotter, with a higher melting temperature, low viscosity, and lets gas escape. It is typical of oceanic crust, ridges and hotspots.

Q: Name the three factors that control magma viscosity and explain why felsic magma is more viscous for each. (1) Temperature: felsic magma is cooler (felsic minerals melt at lower temperature), and cooler melts are more viscous. (2) Silica content: felsic magma has more SiO₄ tetrahedra, which link into tangled chains and sheets. (3) Crystal content: cooler felsic magma already contains crystallized (mafic) minerals, making a thick slurry.

Q: Why do felsic magmas produce explosive eruptions while mafic magmas produce gentle ones? Magma holds dissolved H₂O and CO₂ at high pressure; as it rises the pressure drops and gas exsolves into bubbles (like a shaken soda). In low-viscosity mafic magma the bubbles rise and escape easily, so lava just flows out. In high-viscosity felsic magma the bubbles are trapped, so gas pressure builds until the magma fragments violently. Felsic subduction magmas are also richer in volatiles because the slab supplies water and CO₂, adding to the explosiveness.

Q: Explain Bowen’s reaction series. If you heat a rock with mixed minerals, which melt first? As magma cools, minerals crystallize in order of temperature: mafic (Fe/Mg-rich, e.g., olivine) minerals crystallize first at high temperature; felsic minerals (feldspar, quartz) last at low temperature. Heating reverses this: felsic minerals melt first and mafic minerals last. That’s why rising subduction magma preferentially melts the felsic part of continental crust and becomes felsic.

Q: Why are subduction-zone volcanoes the most dangerous? (Full chain.) The slab brings down water and CO₂ → water lowers the wedge’s melting point → cool, wet magma → it rises through felsic continental crust and melts its felsic parts (Bowen) → the magma becomes felsic: highly viscous and volatile-rich → gas is trapped, then exsolves as pressure drops → explosive Plinian eruptions, pyroclastic flows and lahars from steep stratovolcanoes.

Q: Why are mid-ocean ridge eruptions the least dangerous? They’re mafic (hot, low silica, low viscosity, few volatiles) from decompression melting of dry mantle, so gas escapes easily. They erupt underwater, where high water pressure further suppresses explosions and cold seawater quenches lava into pillow basalts, far from people.

Q: Compare shield volcanoes and stratovolcanoes. Shield volcanoes (Hawaii) are broad and gently sloped, built by runny, low-volatile, high-volume mafic lava flows that travel far, from effusive eruptions, with mostly property hazards. Stratovolcanoes (Fuji, St. Helens) are steep and layered, built from viscous felsic lava that piles up near the vent, interlayered with ash, pyroclastic and lahar deposits from explosive eruptions. They are much more dangerous. Viscosity, volatiles and volume control the shape.

Q: Why do hotspots form islands but mid-ocean ridges mostly don’t? Plumes are hotter than normal upper mantle, so rising plume rock crosses the melting curve deeper and melts more. That large magma supply builds a thick pile of volcanic crust, which by isostasy stands high enough to rise above sea level.

Q: What are pyroclastic flows and lahars, and why are they so deadly? Pyroclastic flows are ground-hugging avalanches of hot gas, ash and rock (from collapsing eruption columns) moving ~300 km/h at hundreds of degrees; you can’t outrun them. Lahars are volcanic mudflows of ash mixed with water (rain, melted snow and ice) that move like fast wet cement down valleys (~50 km/h) and bury towns far from the volcano. Both come from explosive felsic volcanoes and cause most volcanic deaths.

Q: Why are volcanoes scientifically valuable? They bring material up from the mantle we can’t otherwise sample. Ridge basalts come from the shallow mantle and hotspot basalts from the deep mantle, and their chemical differences tell us the mantle is layered and imperfectly mixed.

1(a) Why does the melting temperature of mantle rock change with pressure/depth? Melting means atoms break free of the ordered crystal structure and move apart into a (usually slightly larger-volume) liquid. High pressure squeezes atoms together and resists that, so it takes a higher temperature to melt rock at depth. The melting curve (solidus) therefore slopes to higher temperature with increasing pressure/depth. Lowering the pressure lowers the melting temperature.

1(c) Wet vs. dry plume parcel at the same temperature: which melts deeper? Water lowers the melting temperature at every pressure, so the wet solidus lies to the left (lower T) of the dry solidus. Rising at the same temperature, the parcel’s vertical path crosses the wet solidus first, at greater depth. → The wet parcel melts deeper (and produces more melt).

1(d) Wet mafic magma above a slab: cooler or hotter than dry mafic magma at the same depth? What does it melt in the crust? What volcano forms? Cooler. Water lets the mantle melt at a lower temperature, so the melt forms (and exists) at a lower temperature than dry melt at the same depth. As it rises into crust that is a mix of mafic and felsic rock, it carries only enough heat to melt the lowest-melting-temperature component, which is felsic (Bowen’s reaction series). So the magma becomes increasingly felsic, and the surface volcano is a felsic, explosive stratovolcano.

2(a) Three reasons mafic and felsic magmas have different viscosities 1. Temperature: mafic magmas form and erupt at higher temperatures (mafic minerals melt only at high T), and higher temperature means weaker, more mobile bonds → lower viscosity. Felsic magmas are cooler → more viscous. 2. Silica content: felsic magma has more SiO₂, so more silicate tetrahedra that bond corner-to-corner into long chains and sheets that tangle like bread dough → high viscosity. Mafic magma has less silica and fewer linked chains → runny. 3. Crystal content: cooler felsic magmas contain mafic minerals that have already crystallized (they crystallize first in Bowen’s series), so the magma is a crystal-laden slurry → more viscous. Hot mafic magma has few crystals.

2(b) Why do subduction-zone volcanoes have more volatiles than ridge and hotspot volcanoes? The subducting oceanic plate carries water (locked in minerals altered by seawater and in wet sediments) and CO₂ (carbonate sediments such as limestone) into the mantle. At depth that water and CO₂ are released into the mantle wedge, where they cause melting and dissolve into the magma. Ridges and hotspots melt dry mantle by decompression, with no such source of water and carbon.

2(c) How do viscosity and gas content combine to control explosiveness? Dissolved gas comes out of solution as magma rises and pressure drops. If the magma is runny (mafic), bubbles rise and escape gently → effusive lava flows (even with gas, at most fire fountains). If the magma is viscous (felsic), bubbles can’t escape → gas pressure builds → the magma fragments in an explosive eruption. More gas makes it worse. So: low viscosity + low gas = gentle (Hawaii); high viscosity + high gas = violent (Plinian, St. Helens).

A1. Earth’s mantle is solid rock, yet geophysicists say it convects. Explain what drives mantle convection and give two observations showing that the mantle can flow. > Answer: The mantle is heated from below by the hot core and internally by radioactive decay, and cooled from above. Hot rock expands, becomes less dense and rises; cold rock near the surface becomes denser and sinks. Over geologic time, solid rock under mantle temperature and pressure flows viscously (like Silly Putty pulled slowly) at ~1–10 cm/yr; the huge temperature difference and size of the mantle overcome its high viscosity. Evidence: isostasy (continents and mountains float with deep roots, which requires the mantle to flow out of the way) and post-glacial rebound (land depressed by ice-age ice sheets is still rising thousands of years later, which tells us its viscosity).

A2. Explain why ~90% of an iceberg sits below the waterline. How does the same idea explain why mountain ranges have deep roots? > Answer: Ice is slightly less dense than water (~920 vs. 1,000 kg/m³). A floating object isn’t accelerating, so gravity on it is balanced by buoyancy, which equals the weight of the water it displaces. Because ice is nearly as dense as water, it must submerge ~90% (920/1000) of its volume to displace its own weight. Continental crust (~2,700) floats in denser mantle (~3,300) the same way (isostasy): a mountain range sticking up must be supported by a thick, low-density root extending several times deeper than the mountains are high.

A3. Describe the plate tectonic cycle from the birth of an oceanic plate to its death, and explain what drives plate motion. > Answer: At a mid-ocean ridge, plates move apart; mantle rises passively to fill the gap, melts by decompression, and forms new oceanic crust. The plate moves away, cools, thickens and becomes denser (sinking deeper by isostasy). When old and dense enough, it sinks into the mantle at a subduction zone. The sinking slab pulls the rest of the plate behind it (slab pull), which drives plate motion and opens the ridge. One cycle takes ~200–250 Myr, so seafloor is never older than ~200 Myr. Plate tectonics is the surface expression of mantle convection.

A4. Name three lines of evidence for plate tectonics and explain what each tells us. > Answer (any 3, each with its reasoning): Magnetic stripes: symmetric, parallel bands matching the reversal timescale → new crust is made at ridges and spreads both ways. Deep earthquakes to ~700 km along dipping zones → cold brittle slabs are subducting. Hotspot tracks: island ages increase away from the active hotspot → the plate moves over a fixed plume. Seafloor age/depth/sediment: young, shallow and thin at ridges; older, deeper and thicker away → creation, cooling and recycling. Fit of continents, paleomagnetism, earthquake/volcano distribution, GPS also acceptable.

A5. A fault forms at a divergent plate boundary. What type of fault is it, and how large will its earthquakes typically be? Contrast with a subduction zone. > Answer: Normal fault: plates pulling apart (extension) let the hanging wall drop. Earthquakes are small because the lithosphere is hot and thin (small frictional contact area) and in tension, which rocks resist poorly, so faults slip before much stress builds. At a subduction zone, thrust/reverse faults form under compression; the plates are cold and thick, the contact area is enormous, and rocks are strong in compression, so huge strain builds and is released in the largest earthquakes (M9+).

A6. Using the slider-block demonstration, explain stick-slip behavior and the concept of a seismic gap. > Answer: Steady hand motion = plate motion; bungee/spring = elastic crust; brick on sandpaper = fault held by friction. The brick doesn’t move while the cord stretches and stores elastic energy (stick), then lurches when the pull exceeds friction (slip = earthquake), then sticks again → steady loading produces episodic earthquakes. With two linked bricks, each slip of the first transfers stress to the second, which stays locked until enough stress accumulates. A locked segment whose neighbors have recently ruptured is a seismic gap, the likely site of the next big quake (e.g., near Istanbul on the North Anatolian Fault).

A7. Sketch or describe the particle motion of P, S, Love and Rayleigh waves. Which arrives first, which can’t travel through liquid, and which is most damaging to buildings? Why? > Answer: P: compression/expansion parallel to travel; first. S: shearing perpendicular to travel; not through liquids (no rigidity). Love: surface wave, horizontal side-to-side. Rayleigh: surface wave, rolling ellipse (up-down + back-forth). Love waves are most damaging: surface waves have larger amplitude and lower frequency (resonate with structures), and Love motion is purely horizontal, whereas buildings are designed for vertical (gravity) loads.

A8. Explain how seismologists locate an earthquake using P and S waves. > Answer: P waves are faster than S waves, so the S–P delay grows with distance (like counting between lightning and thunder). At each station, S–P time → distance → a circle of possible locations. Two circles intersect at two points; a third station’s circle selects the single epicenter (trilateration).

A9. Why are subduction-zone volcanoes so much more explosive than Hawaiian volcanoes? > Answer: Hawaii (hotspot): decompression melting of dry, hot plume mantle → mafic magma: hot, low-silica, low viscosity, few volatiles → gas escapes → gentle lava flows, shield volcano. Subduction: slab releases water → flux melting of the wedge → cool, wet magma that rises through continental crust and melts its felsic components (Bowen) → felsic magma: high silica, cool, crystal-rich → high viscosity, and volatile-rich. Rising magma decompresses, gas exsolves (shaken soda) but is trapped → pressure builds → explosive Plinian eruptions, pyroclastic flows, lahars, stratovolcanoes.

A10. Why does the magnitude of an earthquake not by itself determine how much damage it causes? Give two factors with examples. > Answer (any 2–3): Depth (shallow quakes make strong surface waves; deep ones don’t: Venezuela vs. Colombia). Distance (high-frequency waves die out). Key ideas: gravit · collapse · potential · kinetic · heat · fusion

Q4. Use ocean tides to explain how different energy sources for disasters can interact.

Tides are raised by the gravitational pull of the Moon (and Sun) on the oceans and the solid Earth, producing tidal bulges. As Earth rotates through the bulges, orbital/rotational energy is transferred into moving ocean water and slightly heating the solid Earth. On Earth that heat is negligible compared with primordial and radiogenic heat, but on Jupiter’s moon Io, tidal heating is strong enough to power volcanoes: orbital energy becomes “geological” energy.

Key ideas: moon · gravit · orbit · bulge · io

Q5. A house is heated by an oil furnace. What is the ORIGINAL energy source, and why is this a “trick” question?

The Sun. Oil is a fossil fuel made of ancient organisms (plants/plankton) whose energy was captured by photosynthesis. Earth’s heat and pressure only transformed that buried material into oil. Students often say “Earth’s interior” because oil comes out of the ground, but you must trace the energy all the way back.

Key ideas: sun · photosynthesis · fossil · trace

Q6. What is the original energy source for (a) a geyser like Old Faithful, (b) a hydroelectric dam, (c) a windmill, and (d) a nuclear power plant?

  • (a) Geyser: Earth’s interior heat heats groundwater (the water is just the working fluid).
  • (b) Hydroelectric dam: the Sun evaporates water, which falls as rain/snow at high elevation and flows downhill (topography from plate tectonics helps).
  • (c) Windmill: the Sun heats the surface unevenly → atmospheric convection → wind.
  • (d) Nuclear plant: uranium made in stars/supernovae and mined from Earth; not the Sun.

Key ideas: interior · sun · evaporat · convection · supernova

Q7. Compare the energy Earth receives from the Sun with the heat coming out of its interior. Why is this difference relevant to disasters?

Solar input is ~170,000 TW vs. ~46 TW from the interior: the Sun supplies about 4,000× more power. Solar energy drives the vigorous, fast-moving atmosphere and oceans (meteorological disasters: storms, hurricanes, floods), while the much smaller interior heat drives slow mantle convection and plate tectonics (earthquakes, volcanoes).

Key ideas: 170,000 · 46 · atmosphere · mantle

Q8. Define work, energy, force and power, and give the MKS unit of each.

  • Force: a push or pull; F = ma; unit newton (N = kg·m/s²).
  • Work: energy transferred by a force = force × distance; unit joule (J = N·m).
  • Energy: the capacity to do work (or anything that can be turned into heat); unit joule.
  • Power: rate of energy transfer; unit watt (W = J/s).

Key ideas: newton · joule · watt · distance · rate · f = ma

Q9. What is the difference between latent heat and sensible heat? Give an example of each.

  • Sensible heat changes an object’s temperature (heating a pot of water from 20 °C to 80 °C).
  • Latent heat changes its phase without changing temperature (ice melting at 0 °C, water evaporating). The same latent heat is released when vapor condenses or water freezes.

Key ideas: temperature · phase · constant · melt · condens

Q10. What is heat capacity, and why does water’s high heat capacity matter for Earth?

Heat capacity is the amount of heat needed to raise an object’s temperature by 1 degree. Water has a very high heat capacity, so the oceans can store huge amounts of heat with only small temperature changes. That moderates climate but also gives the ocean a large energy reservoir that feeds storms and hurricanes.

Key ideas: 1 degree · store · ocean · storm

Q11. What does conservation of energy mean? A dropped apple stops on the table. Where did its energy go?

Energy is never created or destroyed; it only changes form (potential ↔︎ kinetic ↔︎ heat). The lifted apple has gravitational PE, which becomes KE as it falls; when it thunks onto the table its KE goes into deforming/bruising the apple (and the table) and a little heat and sound.

Key ideas: created · potential · kinetic · heat · deform

Q12. Why do scientists use MKS units and care about significant figures?

MKS (meters, kilograms, seconds) keeps calculations consistent: tracking units tells you how to combine quantities and catches errors. A calculated result can’t be more precise than the least precise input, so reporting 232.282156 m/s for a tsunami speed when depth is known to ~2 figures is false precision; ~230 m/s is right.

Key ideas: meter · kilogram · second · precise · least

Q13. Estimating Earth’s mass as a sphere of rock gives ~3.6 × 10²⁴ kg, but the real value is 6 × 10²⁴ kg. Why is the estimate too low?

The assumption that Earth is all rock fails: Earth differentiated, and its core is iron, much denser than rock. Rocks deep in the mantle are also compressed by enormous pressure, making them denser than surface rock. Both make the true average density higher than ~3,300 kg/m³.

Key ideas: iron · core · dens · pressure

Q14. How does the Sun produce energy, and why will it eventually run out?

Gravitational collapse heated the solar core above ~10⁷ K, hot enough for nuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium, which releases energy and halts further collapse. H–He fusion is slow; the Sun will use up its hydrogen in ~10 billion years (it is ~halfway), then collapse, reheat and fuse heavier elements.

Key ideas: fusion · hydrogen · helium · billion

L4 · Our mobile planet (convection, water, planets) (8 questions)

Q1. Explain how thermal convection gives Earth both a mobile surface and a mobile atmosphere.

Convection: material heated from below expands, becomes less dense and rises; material cooled at the top becomes denser and sinks, setting up circulating cells. - Mantle: heated by the core and radioactive decay, the solid mantle convects slowly (1–10 cm/yr); the cold top layer forms plates that move: plate tectonics (earthquakes, volcanoes). - Atmosphere: the Sun heats the equator more than the poles; air convects, carrying heat poleward: atmospheric circulation (weather, storms). Convection turns heat into motion, and that motion drives disasters.

Key ideas: less dense · rise · sink · mantle · plate · atmospher · sun

Q2. Why does water make Earth’s natural disasters more energetic?

  • High heat capacity: oceans store vast amounts of heat.
  • High latent heat: evaporation absorbs ~23% of solar power and stores it in water vapor; when the vapor condenses in rising air it releases latent heat, warming the air and making it more buoyant, which strengthens the updraft. That is the engine of thunderstorms and hurricanes.
  • Water also amplifies convection through the hydrologic cycle (rivers, erosion, floods) and helps plate tectonics.

Key ideas: heat capacity · latent · evaporat · condens · release · hurricane

Q3. Why does Earth have active plate tectonics while Mars, Mercury and the Moon do not?

Size. Earth is the largest terrestrial planet, so it has retained much of its internal (primordial + radiogenic) heat, keeping its mantle hot enough to convect and break the lithosphere into moving plates. Small bodies lose heat much faster (more surface area relative to volume), so their interiors cooled and convection became weak. Liquid water on Earth (from its orbit) also helps plate tectonics.

Key ideas: size · heat · convect · water

Q4. Explain why Venus, though about Earth’s size, is so different.

Venus is closer to the Sun: its water evaporated and, because water vapor is a greenhouse gas, the heating fed back on itself: a runaway greenhouse (~470 °C surface). Sunlight split H₂O and the hydrogen escaped, so Venus has almost no water, no life, and apparently no plate tectonics. It also spins very slowly (and backward), so it lacks Earth-style cyclonic weather.

Key ideas: sun · runaway · greenhouse · water · spin

Q5. Why are Earth’s orbital position and rotation rate important for disasters?

  • Orbit: Earth is in the habitable zone, so it has liquid water, which drives weather and hurricanes (latent heat), the hydrologic cycle, and helps plate tectonics. Too close = Venus’s runaway greenhouse; too far = frozen Mars.
  • Rotation: Earth spins fast, so the Coriolis effect splits atmospheric circulation into 3 cells per hemisphere, creates jet streams, and makes storms rotate (cyclones/hurricanes). Rotation also drives core flow → magnetic field.

Key ideas: habitable · liquid water · coriolis · cell · cyclon

Q6. How does Earth’s rotation change atmospheric convection?

Without rotation, the Sun-heated equator would drive one big convection cell per hemisphere (rise at the equator, sink at the poles). Earth’s fast spin creates the Coriolis effect, which deflects moving air and breaks it into three cells per hemisphere: Hadley (tropical), Ferrel (mid-latitude), Polar. That produces the trade winds, westerlies and easterlies at the surface and jet streams aloft, and it makes storms rotate.

Key ideas: coriolis · three · hadley · ferrel · polar · jet

Q7. Why is mantle energy release concentrated in particular places rather than spread evenly over the globe? Why does that matter?

Convection focuses heat transport into upwellings (plumes, ridges) and downwellings (subducting slabs). So the ~38 TW of mantle heat is released mainly along plate boundaries and hotspots, which is why earthquakes, volcanoes and mountain building are concentrated in narrow zones, and why plate maps are hazard maps.

Key ideas: upwelling · downwelling · boundar · concentrat

Q8. Describe the mantle: what is it made of, what state is it in, how is it heated, and how fast does it move?

The mantle is ~2,900 km of solid silicate rock between crust and core (~80% of Earth’s volume). It is heated by primordial heat and radioactive decay (and from below by the core). Though solid, it flows very slowly by convection (~1–10 cm/yr, fingernail speed): rock near the surface cools and sinks, rock near the core heats and rises.

Key ideas: solid · 2900 · radioactive · primordial · cm/yr

L5–6 · Plate tectonics, boundaries & evidence (19 questions)

Q1. The mantle is solid rock. What two observations show that it can flow, and what does each tell us?

  • Isostasy: continents and mountains “float” in the denser mantle with deep low-density roots (Himalayan gravity surveys). That balance is only possible if the mantle can flow out of the way, so the mantle can flow.
  • Post-glacial rebound: land pushed down by kilometers of ice during the last ice age (Scandinavia, North America) is still rising ~20,000 years later, like memory foam recovering. The rate of rebound tells us how easily it flows, i.e., the mantle’s viscosity (~10²¹ Pa·s).

Key ideas: isostasy · float · rebound · ice · viscosity

Q2. What does it mean that “plate tectonics is the surface manifestation of mantle convection”?

The mantle convects to get rid of internal heat: hot rock rises, cold rock sinks. The lithosphere is the cold, stiff top boundary layer of that convecting system. Where it is old and cold enough it sinks back into the mantle at subduction zones (the cold downwellings of convection), pulling the plate along; at ridges, mantle wells up to fill the gap. Plate motion is therefore just what convection looks like at the surface.

Key ideas: convect · heat · cold · sink · pull · ridge

Q3. What is the lithosphere, and why does it behave as rigid plates over a mantle that flows?

The lithosphere is the crust plus the uppermost mantle. It is cold, and mantle rock’s viscosity depends strongly on temperature, so cold rock is far stiffer and stronger (molasses in winter). It resists flow and instead breaks (brittle → earthquakes), behaving as rigid plates riding on the hotter, weaker, flowing mantle (asthenosphere) below.

Key ideas: crust · upper · cold · temperature · stiff · asthenosphere

Q4. Describe the plate tectonic cycle from the birth to the death of an oceanic plate.

  1. At a mid-ocean ridge, plates move apart; mantle rises to fill the gap, melts by decompression, and forms new oceanic crust.
  2. The plate moves away (seafloor spreading), possibly carrying a continent.
  3. It cools, thickens and becomes denser, sitting deeper.
  4. When old and dense enough, it sinks into the mantle at a subduction zone; the sinking slab pulls the rest of the plate.
  5. The cycle takes ~200–250 Myr, so oceanic crust is always young (≤ ~200 Myr).

Key ideas: ridge · decompression · cool · dens · subduct · pull

Q5. What drives plate motion?

Mainly slab pull: oceanic lithosphere becomes cold and dense as it ages, so at subduction zones it sinks into the mantle under gravity and drags the attached plate behind it. Mid-ocean ridges are passive: plates are pulled apart and mantle rises to fill the gap (conservation of mass). This is mantle convection with the slabs as cold downwellings.

Key ideas: slab · pull · dens · gravity · passive · convection

Q6. Name the three types of plate boundaries. For each, describe the motion and give an example.

  • Divergent: plates move apart, new lithosphere forms (Mid-Atlantic Ridge/Iceland, East Pacific Rise, East African Rift).
  • Convergent: plates move together; one subducts (Andes, Cascadia, Japan, Aleutians) or continents collide (Himalaya).
  • Transform: plates slide past each other horizontally (San Andreas, North Anatolian Fault).

Key ideas: divergent · convergent · transform · apart · together · slide

Q7. Describe the three types of convergent boundaries and what happens at each.

  • Ocean–ocean: the older, colder, denser oceanic plate subducts → volcanic island arc (Marianas, Aleutians, Japan).
  • Ocean–continent: the oceanic plate subducts beneath the buoyant continent → continental arc (Andes, Cascades).
  • Continent–continent: both are too buoyant to subduct → collision, crustal thickening, mountain building (Himalaya); large earthquakes but no subduction-zone volcanism. Subduction zones (the first two) are the most dangerous boundaries.

Key ideas: ocean-ocean · ocean-continent · continent-continent · denser · buoyant · himalaya

Q8. Why do convergent boundaries produce the largest earthquakes, divergent the smallest, and transforms in between?

Earthquake size depends on how much elastic strain can build before failure, which depends on fault area, rock strength, and stress state. - Divergent: lithosphere is hot and thin (small frictional contact) and in tension; rocks are weak in tension, so they fail early → small quakes. - Transform: moderate temperature/thickness and contact area → intermediate. - Convergent (subduction): plates are cold and thick, the dipping contact is huge, and rocks are in compression (strong) → enormous stored energy → largest (M9+).

Key ideas: hot · thin · tension · cold · thick · compression · contact

Q9. Explain how magnetic stripes on the seafloor provide evidence for seafloor spreading.

Magma erupts at the ridge; as the lava cools, its magnetic minerals lock in the direction of Earth’s magnetic field. The field reverses every ~10,000–100,000 years. As new crust moves away on both sides like two conveyor belts, each polarity period leaves a pair of stripes. The observed stripes are parallel to the ridge, symmetric on either side, and match the dated reversal timescale, which only makes sense if new seafloor is continuously created at the ridge and spreads outward.

Key ideas: cool · magnetic · revers · symmetric · parallel · conveyor

Q10. How does paleomagnetism show that continents have moved?

When rocks form, magnetic minerals align with Earth’s field and freeze in its inclination: horizontal at the equator, steep near the poles. So a rock records the latitude where it formed. If a rock’s frozen field doesn’t match today’s field at its location, it must have formed elsewhere and moved. Dating rocks (radioactive decay) lets us track each continent’s latitude through time (Pangaea ~220 Myr ago → today). It gives latitude, not longitude.

Key ideas: inclination · latitude · freeze · date · moved

Q11. How do hotspot tracks provide evidence for plate motion?

A hotspot is a mantle plume anchored in the deep mantle, roughly fixed. As the plate moves over it, the plume punches through to make a volcano, which is then carried away while a new one forms. The result is a chain of volcanic islands that get progressively older away from the active one (Hawaii → Midway ~27 Myr → bend → Emperor seamounts ~65+ Myr). The age progression gives the plate’s direction and speed.

Key ideas: fixed · plume · older · chain · plate moves

Q12. Why are deep earthquakes (down to ~700 km) strong evidence for subduction?

Hot mantle rock flows rather than breaking, so earthquakes shouldn’t happen at great depth in normal mantle. But deep quakes occur in dipping zones beneath trenches (Wadati–Benioff zones), exactly where plate tectonics predicts cold, brittle oceanic slabs sinking into the mantle. Only a cold subducting slab can host earthquakes that deep: direct evidence of subduction.

Key ideas: 700 · cold · brittle · slab · trench · subduct

Q13. Explain how seafloor age, depth and sediment thickness support plate tectonics.

  • Age: youngest at ridges, older moving away (symmetrically), maximum ~200 Myr. Ocean basins are young and continuously recycled.
  • Depth: shallow at ridges, deeper with age, because the lithosphere cools, contracts and becomes denser, sinking lower by isostasy.
  • Sediment: thin at ridges (new crust), thicker toward trenches (more time to accumulate). All three show crust is created at ridges, ages as it moves away, and is destroyed at subduction zones.

Key ideas: young · old · deep · cool · isostasy · sediment

Q14. Why is the oldest seafloor only ~200 Myr old while continental rocks are ~4 billion years old?

Oceanic lithosphere is constantly created at ridges and destroyed at subduction zones: once it is old, cold and dense enough, it sinks back into the mantle, on a ~200–250 Myr cycle. Continental crust is felsic and low-density (buoyant), so it can’t subduct; it stays at the surface and preserves very old rocks.

Key ideas: recycl · ridge · dens · buoyant · continent

Q15. Why was Wegener’s continental drift rejected, and what changed to make plate tectonics accepted?

Wegener (1915) had good evidence (fitting coastlines, matching fossils and rocks) but no mechanism: geologists couldn’t see how continents could plow through solid rock. After WWII, ocean-floor surveys (bathymetry: ridges, trenches, fracture zones; magnetic stripes) and global seismic networks (built to detect nuclear tests) revealed seafloor spreading, subduction, and earthquake belts. The key insight: continents don’t plow through anything; they ride along inside moving plates, driven by mantle convection.

Key ideas: mechanism · plow · magnetic · seafloor · ride

Q16. How can GPS satellite measurements confirm plate tectonics?

Precise GPS/GNSS stations (not phone GPS) are measured repeatedly over years; they move by a few centimeters per year (~1–10 cm/yr) in directions consistent with plate motion. It is a direct, present-day measurement of plates moving, which wasn’t available when the theory was developed.

Key ideas: gps · cm · direct · year

Q17. Why is plate tectonics important for forecasting and preparing for earthquakes and volcanoes?

It tells us where hazards occur (most earthquakes and volcanoes are on plate boundaries, so plate maps are hazard maps), what kind and how big (boundary type sets fault type, maximum earthquake size and volcano style: subduction zones = largest quakes, tsunamis and explosive volcanoes), and how often in the long term (plate speeds give strain accumulation rates). That guides building codes, warning systems and monitoring, though it can’t predict exact timing.

Key ideas: where · boundar · type · subduction · building

Q18. Why are subduction zones the most dangerous type of plate boundary?

They host the largest earthquakes (M9+ on huge, cold, locked megathrusts in compression), the deepest earthquakes, the deadliest tsunamis (seafloor uplift during thrusting), and the most explosive volcanoes (water from the slab → flux melting → felsic, volatile-rich magma). The Pacific “Ring of Fire” concentrates most of them.

Key ideas: largest · tsunami · explosive · water · ring of fire

Q19. Explain why ocean–continent subduction occurs but continent–continent collisions don’t subduct.

Subduction requires a plate denser than the mantle beneath. Old oceanic lithosphere (mafic crust + cold mantle) becomes dense enough to sink. Continental crust is felsic and low-density, so it is too buoyant to sink; when two continents meet, neither goes down: they crumple, thicken and build mountains (Himalaya).

Key ideas: dens · oceanic · buoyant · felsic · mountain

L7 · Viscosity, convection, rheology & isostasy (12 questions)

Q1. Define viscosity and give its units. Why does a rock fall more slowly through corn syrup than through water?

Viscosity is a fluid’s resistance to flow (stickiness), measured in pascal-seconds (Pa·s). A falling rock feels gravity pulling it down (it’s denser than the fluid) and a viscous drag force resisting its motion; the drag is larger in a more viscous fluid. The rock quickly reaches a terminal velocity where drag balances gravity, and in a high-viscosity fluid that balance happens at a much lower speed. It still sinks eventually because it is denser.

Key ideas: resistance to flow · pa · gravity · drag · terminal

Q2. What is convection, and why does it happen?

Convection is heat transfer by bulk motion of material. Heating makes material expand and become less dense, so it is buoyant and rises; cooling makes it denser, so it sinks. Heat at the bottom and cooling at the top therefore set up circulating cells (lava lamp, pot on a stove, the mantle). It happens when the temperature difference and size are large enough to overcome the material’s viscosity.

Key ideas: less dense · rise · sink · heat · viscosity

Q3. Compare a lava lamp to mantle convection. How is the analogy good, and where does it break down?

Similar: heat at the bottom makes blobs expand, become less dense and rise; they cool at the top, become denser and sink, repeating as convection. Different: (1) the lava lamp uses two different materials (wax and liquid), while rising and sinking mantle rock is mostly the same material at different temperatures; (2) the lava lamp has no plates: its top doesn’t take part, while Earth’s cold top layer (lithosphere) breaks into plates that are part of the convection (slabs sink).

Key ideas: less dense · sink · two · same material · plate

Q4. The mantle’s viscosity (~10²¹ Pa·s) is far higher than peanut butter’s. Why does it still convect?

Whether something convects depends on the temperature difference, the size of the system, thermal properties, and viscosity (high viscosity opposes convection). The mantle has an enormous temperature difference (thousands of °C at the core–mantle boundary vs. ~10 °C at the surface) and is 2,900 km thick. Those factors overwhelm the high viscosity, so it convects, just very slowly (cm/yr) over millions of years.

Key ideas: temperature difference · size · viscosity · slow

Q5. Use Silly Putty to explain the three ways mantle rock can deform, and say where each matters in the Earth.

  • Viscous (pull slowly → it flows like taffy and doesn’t spring back): mantle convection over millions of years.
  • Brittle (yank it fast → it snaps): the cold lithosphere breaking in earthquakes.
  • Elastic (roll it into a ball and bounce it → it deforms and springs back): rocks transmitting seismic waves (elastic waves) and bending before an earthquake.

Key ideas: viscous · brittle · elastic · slow · fast · convection · earthquake · seismic wave

Q6. What two factors control whether rock deforms viscously or brittlely? Use them to explain why Earth has plates.

  • Strain rate: slow deformation → viscous flow; fast → brittle or elastic (Silly Putty).
  • Temperature (most important in the mantle): cold rock is very stiff (slow as molasses in winter), hot rock flows. The cold top of the mantle plus crust (lithosphere) is too stiff to flow and breaks (earthquakes), so it acts as rigid plates; the hot mantle below flows viscously, letting the plates move.

Key ideas: strain rate · temperature · cold · hot · lithosphere · break

Q7. How can the same mantle rock behave as a viscous fluid AND carry elastic seismic waves?

Rheology depends on the timescale (strain rate) of deformation. Over millions of years (convection, post-glacial rebound), mantle rock flows viscously. Over the seconds it takes a seismic wave to pass, it responds elastically: it deforms slightly and springs back, transmitting waves. Silly Putty does the same: it flows if pulled slowly but bounces if dropped.

Key ideas: timescale · million · second · elastic · viscous

Q8. Give a conceptual explanation (no equations) of why ~90% of an iceberg is below the waterline.

Ice (~920 kg/m³) is only slightly less dense than water (~1,000 kg/m³). The iceberg isn’t accelerating, so the downward force of gravity on it must balance the upward buoyant force, which equals the weight of the water it displaces. Since ice is nearly as dense as water, the berg must push aside almost its own volume of water to equal its weight, so ~90% (920/1000 ≈ 92%) sits below the surface and ~10% above. The fraction submerged equals the ratio of the densities.

Key ideas: less dense · buoyan · displace · weight · balance · ratio

Q9. What is isostasy, and how does it explain why mountains have deep roots and continents stand above the seafloor?

Isostasy is the “floating” balance of less-dense crust on denser, flowing mantle: crust sinks until it displaces mantle equal to its own weight (like an iceberg). Continental crust (~2,700 kg/m³) is lighter than mantle (~3,300), so it floats high; a mountain range must be supported by a thick, low-density root extending several times deeper than the peaks are high (4 km mountains → ~18 km root). Felsic continental crust is lighter and thicker than mafic oceanic crust, so continents stand higher.

Key ideas: float · dens · displace · root · mantle

Q10. Give three different places in this course where isostasy explains an observation.

Any three of: - Mountains have deep roots (Himalayan gravity surveys). - Continents stand above the ocean floor (felsic crust is less dense). - Seafloor deepens with age (cooling lithosphere gets denser and sinks lower). - Post-glacial rebound (land rises after the ice load melts). - Hotspot islands (extra melt builds thick crust that pokes above sea level). - Icebergs float ~90% submerged.

Key ideas: mountain · continent · seafloor · rebound · hotspot · iceberg

Q11. Why is the mantle described as a “fluid” but never as a “liquid”?

A fluid is anything that flows when stressed. Over long timescales the mantle’s solid rock flows (convection, isostasy, rebound), so it behaves as a very viscous fluid. But it is not molten: it’s solid crystalline rock, and melt forms only in a few places beneath volcanoes. Calling it liquid is the classic misconception.

Key ideas: flow · solid · not molten · volcano

Q12. A cold slab is denser than the surrounding mantle. Why does it sink only ~10 cm/yr?

Gravity pulls the slab down (it’s denser), but the surrounding mantle is extremely viscous (~10²¹ Pa·s), so the viscous drag balances gravity at a very low terminal velocity. HW2’s force balance gives ~13 cm/yr. A hotter (less viscous) mantle in the past would have let slabs and plates move faster.

Key ideas: viscous · drag · gravity · terminal

L8 · Earthquakes, faults & elastic rebound (8 questions)

Q1. What is an earthquake, and why does it generate seismic waves? Explain elastic rebound theory.

An earthquake is sudden slip of rock across a fault. Plate motion slowly builds stress across a fault that is locked by friction; the rocks on either side bend elastically, storing strain energy. When the stress exceeds the strength/friction of the fault, it ruptures and the rocks snap back (“rebound”) to an unstrained shape in a new, offset position. That sudden release of stored elastic energy radiates as elastic (seismic) waves.

Key ideas: fault · stress · friction · elastic · exceed · rebound · wave

Q2. Describe normal, reverse (thrust) and strike-slip faults. What kind of stress and plate boundary goes with each?

  • Normal: hanging wall moves down relative to the footwall; caused by extension; typical of divergent boundaries (ridges, rifts).
  • Reverse/thrust: hanging wall moves up; caused by compression; typical of convergent boundaries (subduction, collision).
  • Strike-slip: blocks slide horizontally past each other; caused by shear; typical of transform boundaries (San Andreas).

Key ideas: normal · extension · divergent · thrust · compression · convergent · strike-slip · transform

Q3. What is the difference between the hypocenter and the epicenter?

The hypocenter (focus) is the point on the fault where the rupture begins, a location that includes depth. The epicenter is the point on Earth’s surface directly above the hypocenter (its map location). The rupture then spreads across a much larger area of the fault.

Key ideas: start · depth · surface · above

Q4. Why is plotting an earthquake as a dot on a map misleading?

An earthquake starts at the hypocenter but then propagates across a large area of the fault; bigger quakes rupture bigger areas. The 2004 Sumatra–Andaman M9+ quake ruptured ~1,200 km of fault over ~7–8 minutes. Magnitude depends on that rupture area and slip.

Key ideas: propagat · area · bigger · sumatra

Q5. Why can’t foreshocks be used to predict earthquakes?

A foreshock is defined as a smaller quake that precedes a larger mainshock, but there’s no way to tell at the time whether a quake is a foreshock: it is only re-classified as a foreshock after a bigger quake follows. Many small quakes are never followed by a larger one. (Nearly all large quakes have aftershocks, but not all have foreshocks.)

Key ideas: after · larger · mainshock · tell

Q6. Why can you get thrust faults and mountains along a mostly strike-slip fault like the San Andreas?

Real faults aren’t straight. Where a strike-slip fault bends so the two sides push into each other, the bend is in compression, producing reverse/thrust faulting that builds mountains (the Transverse Ranges near LA). Where it bends the other way, the sides pull apart → extension → normal faults and a pull-apart basin (the Dead Sea).

Key ideas: bend · compression · thrust · mountain · extension

Q7. How does a seismometer record ground motion?

It’s essentially a mass on a spring in a frame attached to the ground. When the ground shakes, the frame moves with it, but the mass’s inertia keeps it nearly still; the relative motion between frame and mass is recorded (a seismogram). Modern instruments use three masses to record three components: vertical, north–south and east–west.

Key ideas: mass · spring · inertia · relative · three

Q8. Why do earthquakes occur mainly at plate boundaries, and how does the boundary type determine the kind of earthquake?

Plates move relative to one another, and the stress concentrates where they meet; that’s where rock breaks. The boundary type sets the stress, and the stress sets the fault type: extension at divergent boundaries → normal faults (small quakes); shear at transforms → strike-slip (intermediate); compression at convergent boundaries → thrust (largest, M9 at subduction zones).

Key ideas: boundar · stress · normal · strike-slip · thrust

L8–9, 11 · Seismic waves, seismograms & location (10 questions)

Q1. Describe the particle motion of P, S, Love and Rayleigh waves. Which are body waves and which are surface waves?

  • P (body): compressional/longitudinal; particles move back and forth parallel to the direction of travel (push–pull, like sound).
  • S (body): shear/transverse; particles move perpendicular to travel (up-down or side-to-side, like a shaken rope).
  • Love (surface): horizontal side-to-side shaking perpendicular to travel.
  • Rayleigh (surface): rolling elliptical motion (up-down plus back-forth), like ocean waves.

Key ideas: parallel · perpendicular · body · surface · horizontal · roll

Q2. Which seismic wave arrives first at a seismometer, and why?

The P wave (primary). It is the fastest (~5 km/s in crustal rock vs. ~3 km/s for S) because compressional waves are resisted by both the rock’s resistance to compression and its rigidity, giving a higher effective stiffness. Then come S waves, then the slower surface waves (Love, then Rayleigh).

Key ideas: p wave · fastest · compress · s wave · surface

Q3. Why can’t S waves travel through liquids? What does that tell us about the mantle?

S waves propagate by shearing: each bit of material is displaced sideways and its rigidity (shear stiffness) pulls it back and drags the next bit along. Liquids have no rigidity: they don’t resist shear, so there’s no restoring force (the human-chain demo with arms dropped: nothing propagated). S waves travel through the mantle, which confirms the mantle is solid. (They don’t pass through the liquid outer core.)

Key ideas: shear · rigid · liquid · restor · solid

Q4. Explain why seismic waves are “elastic waves.” How did the Slinky (or human-chain) demo show this?

As a wave passes, rock is compressed or sheared, storing elastic potential energy, then springs back to its original shape (like a stretched Slinky), pushing on the next bit of rock, which gains kinetic energy. The wave travels by this continual exchange of elastic PE and KE; the rock isn’t permanently deformed. In the human chain, each person moved only because the person next to them pushed or pulled, not by anticipating.

Key ideas: elastic · spring back · potential · kinetic · slinky

Q5. Compare body waves and surface waves: speed, frequency, amplitude, and damage.

Body waves (P, S) travel through Earth’s interior; they are faster and higher frequency (short period) and smaller in amplitude. Surface waves (Love, Rayleigh) are trapped near the surface; they are slower, lower frequency (long period), and larger in amplitude because their energy stays concentrated at the surface. They are more damaging: bigger shaking, periods that match buildings, and they travel far.

Key ideas: faster · slower · frequency · amplitude · damag

Q6. Describe what a seismogram of a distant large earthquake looks like and how you would identify each wave.

Three traces (vertical, N–S, E–W). First, a small, high-frequency P wave, mostly on the vertical component (waves arrive steeply from below). Minutes later, the larger S wave, mostly on the horizontal components. Then the large, long-period surface waves: the Love wave (horizontal, perpendicular to the path) followed by the Rayleigh wave (vertical plus horizontal along the path). Shaking can continue for hours as waves circle Earth.

Key ideas: p · vertical · s · horizontal · love · rayleigh · period

Q7. Explain how seismologists locate an earthquake using P and S waves.

  1. P waves travel faster than S waves, so the S–P delay grows with distance (like counting seconds between lightning and thunder).
  2. At each station, measure the S–P time and convert it (using known speeds/travel-time curves) to a distance.
  3. Draw a circle of that radius around the station.
  4. Two stations give two possible intersection points; a third station picks the single point where all circles meet: the epicenter (trilateration).

Key ideas: faster · s-p · distance · circle · three

Q8. How is locating an earthquake like figuring out how far away lightning struck?

Light arrives almost instantly but thunder (sound) travels ~0.2 miles per second, so counting the seconds between flash and thunder gives distance (~5 s per mile). Similarly, P waves outrun S waves, so the S–P delay gives distance to the quake. With three observers/stations, the three distance circles intersect at one point: the location. The difference is that P is only ~2× faster than S, while light is vastly faster than sound.

Key ideas: light · sound · delay · distance · three

Q9. Why do you need at least three seismic stations to locate an earthquake?

One station’s S–P time gives only a distance, so the quake could be anywhere on a circle around it. A second station’s circle intersects the first at two points, which is still ambiguous. A third circle passes through only one of those points, uniquely fixing the epicenter.

Key ideas: distance · circle · two points · third

Q10. Define amplitude, wavelength, period and frequency.

  • Amplitude: the size (height) of the wave, i.e., how far the ground is displaced.
  • Wavelength: distance between successive crests.
  • Period: time between successive crests passing a point.
  • Frequency: number of waves per second = 1/period.

Key ideas: amplitude · wavelength · period · frequency · 1/period

L9–10 · Magnitude, intensity & building damage (12 questions)

Q1. What does it mean that earthquake magnitude scales are logarithmic? Compare an M7 and an M9.

Each step of 1 in magnitude means 10× larger wave amplitude (shaking) and about 32× more energy. An M9 shakes the ground with ~100× the amplitude of an M7 and releases ~1,000× (32²) as much energy. (HW: 3 cm road waves at M7 → 30 cm at M8 → 3 m at M9.)

Key ideas: 10 · 32 · 100 · 1000 · amplitude

Q2. What is the difference between earthquake magnitude and intensity?

Magnitude measures the size of the earthquake itself (energy released, from rupture area × slip or wave amplitudes); there is one value per quake, on a logarithmic scale. Intensity (Mercalli scale, I–XII) measures how strongly the ground shook at a particular location, so one quake has many intensities. Intensity depends on magnitude, distance, depth, duration and local geology.

Key ideas: size · one · location · mercalli · distance · geology

Q3. Compare the Richter scale with the moment magnitude scale.

The Richter scale (the first one) uses the logarithm of the peak amplitude of waves on a seismogram, corrected for distance. It underestimates very large or distant earthquakes. The moment magnitude scale is based on the seismic moment = rock strength (rigidity) × rupture area × slip, i.e., the physical size of the rupture and the energy released. It is the modern, most accurate scale, especially for great earthquakes. Both are logarithmic.

Key ideas: amplitude · underestimat · moment · area · slip · logarithm

Q4. What controls an earthquake’s magnitude? Why is there no magnitude 10?

Magnitude depends on the area of fault that ruptures (length × depth extent), the amount of slip, and the strength/friction of the rock. Bigger faults and more slip mean bigger quakes: ~100 km rupture ≈ M7, ~1,000+ km ≈ M8–9. The largest recorded is M9.5 (Chile 1960, ~1,500 km). An M10 would need a fault far longer than any on Earth.

Key ideas: area · slip · fault · 9.5 · no fault

Q5. Why are Love waves more damaging to buildings than Rayleigh waves?

Buildings are engineered to carry vertical loads (their own weight under gravity), so vertical shaking is relatively safe. Love waves are entirely horizontal: they shove the foundation sideways while the building’s mass lags (inertia), shearing columns and toppling structures (especially soft first stories). Rayleigh waves are mostly vertical rolling motion, which buildings tolerate better.

Key ideas: horizontal · vertical · gravity · designed · shear

Q6. Give three reasons surface waves cause more damage than body waves.

  1. Larger amplitude: their energy stays concentrated near the surface where buildings are.
  2. Lower frequency (longer period): they match the natural sway periods of buildings and bridges → resonance, and low-frequency waves travel farther without dying out.
  3. Horizontal motion (especially Love waves) that buildings aren’t designed to resist.

Key ideas: amplitude · frequency · resonan · horizontal · farther

Q7. Explain resonance and why it matters for building damage.

Every building sways back and forth at a natural period (~0.1 s for a 1-story house, ~3 s for a 30-story tower; flexible materials sway slower). If incoming seismic waves have about the same period, each push adds to the sway and the shaking is amplified: resonance, a common cause of catastrophic failure. Long-period surface waves from large quakes tend to resonate with tall buildings and bridges.

Key ideas: natural period · match · amplif · tall

Q8. What is site amplification? Explain why it happens and give an example.

Ground built on soft sediments, mud, artificial fill or old lake beds shakes much more than nearby bedrock during the same earthquake. Seismic waves slow down in soft material; to conserve energy their amplitude increases, and sediment-filled basins can resonate (“shake like a bowl of Jell-O”). Examples: Mexico City 1985 (old lake bed, 350+ km from the epicenter, ~8,000 dead); the Nimitz Freeway collapse in the 1989 Loma Prieta quake (built on mud); 1906 San Francisco damage on fill.

Key ideas: soft · bedrock · slow · amplitude · mexico

Q9. How do rupture length and duration affect the frequency of seismic waves, and how does frequency affect where damage occurs?

Short ruptures (small quakes) radiate mostly high-frequency waves; long, long-lasting ruptures (big quakes) radiate lots of low-frequency waves. High-frequency waves damage structures near the epicenter but die out quickly with distance. Low-frequency waves travel much farther and resonate with tall buildings and bridges, so great earthquakes can cause damage far away.

Key ideas: short · long · high frequency · low frequency · die out · far

Q10. Why are deep earthquakes usually less damaging than shallow ones of the same magnitude?

Shallow earthquakes (within a few tens of km of the surface) generate strong surface waves, which have large amplitudes and low frequencies and do most of the damage. Deep earthquakes (e.g., 100–700 km) generate few surface waves, and the energy spreads out over a longer path before reaching the surface. Example: the deep (110 km) 2026 Colombia M7.4 was much less destructive than the shallow Venezuela M7.2/7.5 doublet.

Key ideas: shallow · deep · surface wave · distance

Q11. List factors besides magnitude that determine how much damage an earthquake causes.

  • Depth (shallow → strong surface waves).
  • Distance from population centers (high frequencies decay).
  • Local geology / site amplification (soft sediment vs. bedrock).
  • Wave frequency vs. building resonance.
  • Duration of shaking.
  • Construction quality and building codes (Haiti vs. Chile).
  • Secondary hazards: tsunamis, fires, landslides.
  • Warning systems and hazard communication (Sumatra 2004, Fukushima, L’Aquila).

Key ideas: depth · distance · site · resonan · construction · tsunami · warning

Q12. A smaller earthquake in Virginia (M5.8) was felt over a much larger area than a larger one in California (M6.0). Why?

Intensity depends on the local geology, not just magnitude. Eastern North America’s crust is old and cold, far from any active plate boundary, and transmits seismic energy very efficiently with little loss, so the waves are felt far away. California’s warmer, more fractured crust near an active boundary absorbs energy faster. “The geology matters.”

Key ideas: old · cold · transmit · geology · intensity

L10 · Boundary types & famous earthquakes (12 questions)

Q1. For a divergent plate boundary, what type of faulting and what size earthquakes do you expect? Why? (She said she wants you able to explain this.)

Normal faulting, because the plates are pulling apart (extension), so the hanging wall drops down. Earthquakes are small, because new lithosphere at a spreading center is hot and thin (small frictional contact area) and the rock is in tension, which it can’t sustain much of, so faults slip before much stress builds. (Continental rifts, with thicker lithosphere, can be somewhat larger.)

Key ideas: normal · extension · small · hot · thin · tension

Q2. For a convergent (subduction) boundary, what type of faulting and what size earthquakes do you expect? Why?

Thrust (reverse) faulting, because the plates are pushed together (compression), so the hanging wall moves up (the overriding plate rides over the subducting one on the megathrust). Earthquakes are the largest (up to M9+), because the plates are cold and thick, the dipping contact area is enormous (hundreds to >1,000 km long), and rocks are strong in compression, so huge elastic energy builds before release. They also generate tsunamis.

Key ideas: thrust · compression · largest · cold · thick · area · tsunami

Q3. For a transform boundary, what type of faulting and what size earthquakes do you expect? Why?

Strike-slip faulting: the plates slide horizontally past each other (shear). Earthquakes are intermediate: they can be large and very deadly (Haiti 2010 M7.0, Turkey 2023 M7.8, San Francisco 1906 ~M7.9) but not M9, because the near-vertical fault is relatively narrow in depth (moderate temperature and thickness, modest contact area).

Key ideas: strike-slip · horizontal · intermediate · not m9

Q4. What concept(s) does the 2009 L’Aquila, Italy earthquake illustrate?

  1. Normal faulting can occur within a convergent setting: Italy sits above a subduction zone, but the overriding plate bends/buckles, creating extension and normal faults. (2) Communicating risk: after many small tremors, officials told residents not to worry; an M6.3 then killed ~300 people in old, vulnerable buildings, and scientists were prosecuted (convicted, later acquitted), a lesson about how hazard information is communicated.

Key ideas: normal · extension · subduction · communicat · building

Q5. Why was the 2010 Haiti earthquake (M7.0) one of the deadliest in history?

It was a “medium-sized” strike-slip (transform) quake, ~100× smaller in amplitude than an M9, but it was shallow, directly beneath a densely populated city (Port-au-Prince), and buildings were poorly constructed with weak or unenforced building codes. ~150,000+ people died. Size isn’t everything: depth, proximity and construction matter.

Key ideas: strike-slip · shallow · city · construction · size

Q6. Why did the 2004 Sumatra–Andaman earthquake kill so many people?

It was a huge (M9.1–9.3) subduction megathrust quake that ruptured ~1,200 km of fault, lifting the seafloor (~20 m uplift) and generating a tsunami that crossed the Indian Ocean. Most of ~230,000 deaths were from the tsunami because there was no Indian Ocean tsunami warning system (unlike the Pacific), it struck on a holiday, and scientists had underestimated the chance of an M9 in that subduction zone; now we think an M9 is possible at almost any subduction zone.

Key ideas: tsunami · warning · subduction · underestimat · holiday

Q7. What does the 2011 Tohoku earthquake and the Fukushima disaster illustrate?

Even a well-prepared country can suffer when hazard knowledge isn’t used: most of the ~16,000–18,000 deaths were from the tsunami, and the Fukushima nuclear plant failed. Seismologists knew an M9 was possible there, but that wasn’t communicated to those designing seawalls and the plant. Also: well-constructed buildings with roof refuges (a coastal school) saved lives.

Key ideas: tsunami · fukushima · communicat · known · seawall

Q8. The 1985 Mexico City earthquake illustrates two important concepts. What are they?

  1. Seismic gap: the Michoacán segment of the Cocos–North America subduction zone hadn’t ruptured in a long time while its neighbors had; the 1985 M8.1 “filled the gap.”
  2. Site amplification: Mexico City is 350–400 km from the epicenter but is built on the soft sediments of an old lake bed (the drained Aztec lake). Those sediments amplified and prolonged the shaking (“bowl of Jell-O”); hard-rock sites shook little, and ~8,000 died in collapses.

Key ideas: seismic gap · site amplification · lake · sediment · far

Q9. Why do seismologists expect a magnitude ~9 earthquake in Cascadia even though it has been quiet in recorded history?

Cascadia is a subduction zone (Juan de Fuca plate under North America), and subduction zones host the largest earthquakes. The megathrust is locked, so its quiet means strain is accumulating (elastic rebound), not that it’s safe. Geologic evidence (ghost forests, tree rings, oral histories, offshore deposits, and the 1700 “orphan tsunami” in Japanese records) shows it produced an M9 on Jan 26, 1700 and many before. The next one is inevitable, but its timing can’t be predicted.

Key ideas: subduction · locked · strain · 1700 · inevitable

Q10. How do we know the date of the last great Cascadia earthquake (January 26, 1700)?

From written records in Japan of an “orphan tsunami” (a tsunami with no local earthquake), which modeling matched to an M9 rupture of Cascadia. Supporting evidence: ghost forests (coastal trees drowned when the land subsided; tree rings show they died between 1699 and 1700) and Native oral histories, which narrow the time but don’t give the exact date.

Key ideas: japan · tsunami · tree · oral

Q11. Why did the Nimitz Freeway collapse in the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake?

The collapsed section of the double-decker freeway (Cypress viaduct) was built on soft mud, which amplified the shaking (site amplification). The structure also had a natural period that resonated with the shaking of the muds, so the upper deck pancaked onto the lower deck, killing 42 of the quake’s 67 victims. Nearby sections on firmer ground survived.

Key ideas: mud · amplif · resonan · pancake

Q12. Why do strong earthquakes in Chile cause fewer deaths than smaller ones in some other countries?

Chile has strict, well-enforced building codes and good infrastructure, so buildings survive strong shaking (the 2010 M8.8 and 2015 M8.3 had relatively low fatalities). Places with poor construction (Haiti 2010, M7.0) suffer far more. There is no one-to-one relationship between magnitude and fatalities.

Key ideas: building code · infrastructure · magnitude · haiti

L11 · Stick-slip, slider block & seismic gaps (5 questions)

Q1. Explain how the slider-block demonstration illustrates stick-slip behavior and the earthquake cycle.

The hand moving steadily represents plate motion; the bungee cord is the elastic crust; the brick on sandpaper is the fault held by friction. As the hand moves, the brick doesn’t move; the cord stretches and stores elastic energy (stick). When the cord’s pull exceeds friction, the brick suddenly lurches forward (slip = the earthquake), releasing the stored energy; then it sticks again and the cycle repeats. So steady plate motion produces episodic, sudden earthquakes, which is elastic rebound.

Key ideas: plate · elastic · friction · store · lurch · repeat

Q2. How does the two-block (two-brick) slider demo illustrate a seismic gap?

Two bricks linked by a cord represent two adjacent fault segments. Pulling on the first, it slips repeatedly (small quakes); each slip stretches the cord to the second brick, transferring stress to it. The second brick stays locked until enough stress accumulates to overcome its friction, then it slips (often right after a first-brick slip). Before it moves, it is a seismic gap: a segment that hasn’t ruptured but is being loaded and is “ready to go.”

Key ideas: two · transfer · locked · gap · friction

Q3. What is a seismic gap? Give an example and explain why it matters for hazards.

A seismic gap is a segment of an active fault (often a subduction zone or major transform) that hasn’t ruptured in a long time while neighboring segments have. It is still being loaded by plate motion and by stress transferred from neighbors, so it is the most likely place for the next large earthquake. Examples: the segment of the North Anatolian Fault near Istanbul, the Shumagin Gap in Alaska, Cascadia. Gaps tell us where to prepare, though not exactly when.

Key ideas: hasn’t ruptured · neighbor · load · next · istanbul

Q4. If plates move steadily at a few cm/yr, why do earthquakes happen suddenly instead of the faults sliding smoothly?

Most faults have high friction, so they stick while the plates keep moving, and the surrounding rock bends elastically, storing strain. Only when the stored stress exceeds the frictional strength does the fault slip suddenly, releasing years to centuries of accumulated motion at once. Faults with low friction can creep steadily instead (part of the San Andreas), producing only small quakes.

Key ideas: friction · stick · elastic · exceed · creep

Q5. Why do earthquakes on long faults often occur in sequences that march along the fault?

When one segment ruptures, it relieves its own stress but transfers stress to adjacent segments (like the first brick loading the second through the cord). The neighboring segment is pushed closer to failure and often ruptures years to decades later. The North Anatolian Fault ruptured westward in a sequence from 1939 to 1999, leaving the remaining gap near Istanbul.

Key ideas: transfer · adjacent · segment · anatolian

L12 · Volcanic settings & why the mantle melts (12 questions)

Q1. In what three tectonic settings does almost all volcanism occur? Describe the eruptions in each.

  1. Divergent boundaries (mid-ocean ridges): mostly underwater, non-explosive eruptions of pillow basalt; ~80% of Earth’s magma; least dangerous.
  2. Convergent boundaries (subduction zones): volcanic arcs (Cascades, Andes, Japan) with the most explosive and dangerous eruptions.
  3. Hotspots (mantle plumes), usually intraplate: abundant, gentle mafic eruptions that build ocean islands (Hawaii).

Key ideas: divergent · subduction · hotspot · pillow · explosive · island

Q2. Most of the mantle is solid. Explain why melting occurs beneath mid-ocean ridges and hotspots.

The melting temperature of mantle rock increases with pressure (pressure makes it harder for atoms to break their bonds and become liquid). Beneath ridges (plates pulled apart → mantle rises to fill the gap) and hotspots (buoyant plumes), hot solid mantle rises. It barely cools, but the pressure drops, so its melting temperature falls below its actual temperature: it crosses the melting curve and partially melts without any heat being added. This is decompression (pressure-release) melting.

Key ideas: pressure · melting temperature · rise · decompression · without

Q3. Subduction zones are colder than average mantle. Why do they have volcanoes?

The subducting slab carries water (in hydrated minerals formed by seawater alteration) and CO₂ down. As the slab heats at depth, the water is driven off (“cooked out”) into the hot overlying mantle wedge. Water weakens silicate bonds and lowers the melting temperature, shifting the melting curve to lower temperatures, so the wedge, whose temperature hasn’t changed, now melts. This is dehydration (flux) melting, like salt melting ice on a sidewalk.

Key ideas: water · slab · wedge · lower · melting temperature · dehydration · salt

Q4. Correct this statement: “Subduction-zone volcanoes form because the slab sinks into the hot mantle, heats up, and melts.”

Wrong on two counts: subduction zones are colder than average mantle (cold slabs cool it, like ice in a drink), and melting is not caused by heating. The slab releases water into the overlying mantle wedge, and water lowers the wedge’s melting temperature so it melts at its existing temperature (dehydration/flux melting). It’s mainly the wedge, not the slab, that melts.

Key ideas: colder · water · wedge · lower · melting temperature

Q5. Use a temperature–pressure (phase) diagram to explain the two ways the mantle melts.

Axes: temperature (x), pressure/depth (y, increasing downward). The melting curve (solidus) slopes to higher temperature at greater depth; solid is on the cool/high-pressure side, liquid on the hot/low-pressure side. - Decompression: a hot parcel rises vertically (same T, lower P) and crosses the solidus into the melt field (ridges, hotspots). - Flux/dehydration: adding water shifts the solidus to lower temperatures (left); a rock that was solid now lies on the melt side of the wet solidus without moving (subduction zones).

Key ideas: temperature · pressure · solidus · rise · water · shift

Q6. Why do hotspots form islands like Hawaii while mid-ocean ridges mostly don’t rise above sea level?

Mantle plumes are hotter than the shallow mantle under ridges, so the rising plume crosses the melting curve deeper and melts over a longer column, producing more melt (higher magma flux per area). That builds a thick pile of volcanic crust, which by isostasy stands high enough to emerge above sea level as an island.

Key ideas: hotter · deeper · more melt · thick · isostasy

Q7. Why do transform boundaries have essentially no volcanoes?

At transforms, plates slide horizontally past each other. There’s no upwelling of mantle (so no decompression melting, unlike ridges) and no water being carried down (so no flux melting, unlike subduction zones). With nothing to make the mantle melt, there is no volcanism. (The movie “Volcano,” with lava in LA, is nonsense.)

Key ideas: horizontal · upwell · decompression · water · no melt

Q8. Why are mid-ocean ridge eruptions the least explosive and dangerous?

Ridge magma comes from decompression melting of dry, shallow mantle, so it is mafic: hot, low-silica, low viscosity and volatile-poor, and gas escapes easily. Eruptions occur underwater, where the high water pressure further suppresses gas exsolution and cold seawater quenches the lava into pillow basalts, far from people.

Key ideas: decompression · mafic · low viscosity · volatile · underwater · pillow

Q9. Why are volcanoes scientifically valuable? What do ridge and hotspot lavas tell us?

Volcanoes are “messengers from the mantle”: they carry mantle-derived material to the surface, one of the only ways to sample the mantle (~80% of Earth’s volume). Mid-ocean ridge basalts come from passive upwelling of the shallow mantle; hotspot basalts come from plumes rising from the deep mantle near the core. Both are basalts, but their trace-element chemistry differs, showing the deep and shallow mantle are chemically different and not fully mixed.

Key ideas: sample · shallow · deep · chemistry · mantle

Q10. How does mantle convection relate to where volcanoes form?

The solid mantle convects to lose heat, and heat transfer is focused into downwellings (subducting slabs) and upwellings (plumes; passive upwelling at ridges). Volcanism happens where convection brings mantle up (decompression at ridges and plumes) or carries water down (flux melting above slabs). So volcanoes mark the up- and downwellings of mantle convection.

Key ideas: convect · slab · plume · ridge · water

Q11. Why does mantle rise beneath mid-ocean ridges? Is it pushed up?

No. Spreading is passive: plates are pulled apart mainly by slabs sinking at subduction zones elsewhere. As the plates separate, mantle must rise to fill the gap (conservation of mass), drawing up shallow mantle that then decompresses and melts. (Plumes, by contrast, rise actively because they’re hot and buoyant.)

Key ideas: passive · pulled · fill · decompress

Q12. What is the difference between island arcs and continental arcs?

Both are lines of volcanoes on the overriding plate above a subduction zone, parallel to the trench. An island arc forms where ocean subducts beneath ocean (Japan, Aleutians, Philippines, Marianas). A continental arc forms where ocean subducts beneath a continent (Cascades, Andes). Continental-arc magmas pass through more felsic crust, so they tend to be more felsic; both are explosive.

Key ideas: ocean · continent · subduct · felsic · japan · cascade

L13 · Silicates, Bowen, felsic vs mafic, viscosity, volatiles (11 questions)

Q1. Why do oxygen and silicon form silicate tetrahedra, and why does this matter for magma behavior?

O and Si are the two most abundant crustal elements. Silicon needs 4 electrons to fill its outer shell, so it bonds with four oxygens, arranged as a tetrahedron (maximizing the distance between electron pairs) = SiO₄. Each corner oxygen still needs one more electron, so tetrahedra bond to metals (making minerals) or to each other at their corners, forming chains and sheets. The more silica a melt has, the more linked chains it contains, which makes it stickier (more viscous).

Key ideas: oxygen · silicon · four · tetrahedr · chain · viscous

Q2. Explain the differences between felsic and mafic rocks and magmas.

  • Felsic (feldspar + silica): silica-rich (>65% SiO₂), lighter elements (Si, O, K, Na), low density, low melting temperature, cooler magma, high viscosity, traps volatiles; typical of continental crust and subduction-zone magmas (granite, rhyolite).
  • Mafic (magnesium + iron): silica-poor, rich in Fe and Mg, high density, high melting temperature, hotter magma, low viscosity, gas escapes; typical of oceanic crust, ridges and hotspots (basalt, gabbro).

Key ideas: silica · density · melting · viscosity · continent · ocean · iron

Q3. Explain Bowen’s reaction series. If you heat a rock containing many different minerals, which melt first?

Bowen’s reaction series is the order in which minerals crystallize from a cooling magma: mafic (Fe/Mg-rich) minerals like olivine and pyroxene crystallize first, at the highest temperatures; felsic minerals (feldspar, mica, quartz) crystallize last, at the lowest temperatures. Heating reverses the order: felsic minerals melt first (lowest melting temperature) and mafic minerals melt last.

Key ideas: crystalliz · mafic · first · felsic · last · temperature · melt first

Q4. Name the three factors that control magma viscosity, and explain why each makes felsic magma more viscous than mafic magma.

  1. Temperature: higher temperature breaks bonds and lets molecules move → lower viscosity. Mafic magmas are hotter (mafic material only melts at high T); felsic magmas are cooler → more viscous.
  2. Silica content: silicate tetrahedra link into tangled chains and sheets (like bread dough or egg whites). Felsic magma has more silica → more viscous.
  3. Crystal content: crystals make a thicker slurry. Cooler felsic magma contains mafic minerals that have already crystallized (Bowen) → more viscous; hot mafic magma has few crystals.

Key ideas: temperature · silica · crystal · cooler · chain · bowen

Q5. What are volatiles, why does pressure matter, and how do volatiles behave in felsic vs. mafic magma?

Volatiles are gases dissolved in magma, mainly H₂O and CO₂. Their solubility increases with pressure: deep in the chamber, magma holds lots of gas; as it rises, pressure drops and the gas comes out of solution as bubbles, like opening a shaken soda bottle. In runny mafic magma, bubbles escape easily → gentle eruptions. In sticky felsic magma, bubbles are trapped; gas pressure builds until the magma fragments explosively.

Key ideas: h2o · co2 · pressure · bubble · soda · trap

Q6. Why do subduction-zone magmas contain more volatiles than mid-ocean ridge or hotspot magmas?

The subducting slab carries water (bound in minerals altered by seawater and in wet sediments) and CO₂ (in carbonate sediments like limestone) into the mantle. These are released into the mantle wedge, where they trigger melting and dissolve into the magma. Ridges and hotspots melt dry mantle by decompression, with no comparable source of water or carbon.

Key ideas: slab · water · co2 · seawater · dry · decompression

Q7. Explain how a magma that starts as wet basalt above a subducting slab ends up felsic.

Water lowers the melting point, so the wedge melt is cool and wet (initially mafic). As it rises through continental crust, which contains felsic material, it has enough heat to melt only the crust’s lowest-melting-temperature components, and by Bowen’s reaction series those are the felsic minerals. The magma absorbs that silica-rich melt and becomes increasingly felsic by the time it reaches the volcano. (Island arcs have less crust to melt, so they’re less felsic than continental arcs.)

Key ideas: cool · wet · continental crust · felsic · bowen · silica

Q8. How does the combination of viscosity and volatile content determine how explosive an eruption is?

Gas comes out of solution as magma rises and decompresses. Low viscosity + few volatiles (mafic, ridges/hotspots): bubbles escape easily, so lava flows out gently. High viscosity + many volatiles (felsic, subduction zones): bubbles are trapped, pressure builds until the magma fragments, giving an explosive eruption. More gas or stickier magma → more explosive.

Key ideas: viscosity · volatile · escape · trap · explosive · mafic · felsic

Q9. Describe the three basic rock types.

  • Igneous (“fire”): solidified magma or lava. Intrusive/plutonic (cools slowly underground → coarse crystals, e.g., granite) or extrusive/volcanic (cools fast at the surface → fine-grained, e.g., basalt).
  • Sedimentary: grains eroded from rocks and carried by water, ice or wind, then deposited and cemented (sandstone, shale).
  • Metamorphic: existing rock transformed by heat, pressure or fluids without melting (shale → schist → gneiss).

Key ideas: igneous · sedimentary · metamorphic · magma · erod · without melting

Q10. Why are intrusive igneous rocks coarse-grained and extrusive rocks fine-grained?

Intrusive (plutonic) magma freezes underground, surrounded by warm rock, so it cools slowly, giving crystals time to grow large (coarse-grained granite, El Capitan, East Rock). Extrusive (volcanic) lava freezes at the cold surface (or in water), so it cools quickly and crystals stay tiny (fine-grained basalt, pillow basalts).

Key ideas: slow · underground · fast · surface · large · small

Q11. Why does felsic continental crust stand above sea level while mafic oceanic crust lies below it?

Felsic rock is rich in lighter elements (Si, O) and has lower density than mafic rock (rich in heavy Fe, Mg). By isostasy, lighter, thicker continental crust floats higher on the mantle, while denser, thinner oceanic crust sits lower. Without felsic magmas, “we’d all be swimming.”

Key ideas: felsic · density · mafic · isostasy

L14 · Eruption styles, flows & volcano types (12 questions)

Q1. Compare non-explosive and explosive eruption styles. What kind of magma produces each, and why?

  • Non-explosive (Hawaiian/Icelandic fire fountains and basalt lava flows; Strombolian bursts): produced by mafic magma, hot, low silica, low viscosity, few volatiles, so gas escapes easily and lava flows.
  • Explosive (Vulcanian, Plinian, caldera): produced by felsic magma, cool, silica-rich, high viscosity, volatile-rich, so gas is trapped until pressure fragments the magma into ash and pumice, sending columns tens of km high.

Key ideas: mafic · felsic · viscosity · volatile · lava flow · plinian

Q2. Why are subduction-zone volcanoes the most explosive and dangerous? Give the whole causal chain.

Slab carries water and CO₂ down → water released into the mantle wedge lowers its melting point (dehydration/flux melting) → cool, wet magma → rising through felsic continental crust, it melts the felsic (lowest-melting) components (Bowen) → magma becomes felsic: high silica + cooler + more crystals = high viscosity, plus lots of volatiles → as it rises, pressure drops and gas exsolves (shaken soda) but is trapped → pressure builds → explosive Vulcanian/Plinian/caldera eruptions, pyroclastic flows, lahars, steep stratovolcanoes.

Key ideas: water · melting point · felsic · viscous · volatile · trap · pyroclastic

Q3. Why are Hawaiian eruptions gentle even though Hawaii has very active volcanoes?

Hawaii is a hotspot: magma comes from decompression melting of a hot, dry plume, so it is mafic (basalt): hot, low-silica, low viscosity, with few volatiles (no subducting slab to supply water). Any gas bubbles escape easily, so instead of exploding the lava erupts as fire fountains and flowing lava (pahoehoe, a’a). Flows threaten property, not usually lives.

Key ideas: hotspot · decompression · mafic · low viscosity · volatile · escape · flow

Q4. Compare shield volcanoes and stratovolcanoes: magma, setting, shape, eruptions and hazards.

  • Shield: mafic magma at hotspots (Hawaii) and ridges (Iceland). Broad, gently sloping because runny, low-volatile, high-volume lava flows far before solidifying. Effusive eruptions (lava flows); mainly property damage.
  • Stratovolcano/composite: felsic magma at subduction zones (Fuji, St. Helens). Steep, layered cone because viscous lava piles up near the vent, interlayered (“strata”) with ash, pyroclastic and lahar deposits from explosive eruptions. Hazards: pyroclastic flows, lahars, ash; deadliest.

Key ideas: shield · stratovolcano · mafic · felsic · broad · steep · layer

Q5. What are the “three V’s,” and how do they control volcano shape and eruption style?

Viscosity, volatiles and volume. Low-viscosity, low-volatile magma (mafic) flows easily and lets gas escape → effusive eruptions; with high volume it builds broad shield volcanoes. High-viscosity, volatile-rich magma (felsic) piles up and traps gas → explosive eruptions → steep, layered stratovolcanoes. Viscosity and volatiles are the key concepts.

Key ideas: viscosity · volatile · volume · shield · strato · explosive

Q6. What are pyroclastic flows and lahars, and why are they the deadliest volcanic hazards?

  • Pyroclastic flows (nuées ardentes): when an explosive eruption column collapses (or blasts sideways), a ground-hugging avalanche of hot gas, ash and rock races downslope at ~300 km/h and hundreds of °C. It is impossible to outrun and kills everything in its path (Mt. Pelée 1902, ~30,000 in ~10 s).
  • Lahars: volcanic ash and debris mixed with water (rain, rivers, melting snow and glaciers) become a fast mudflow like wet cement (~50 km/h) that follows valleys and buries towns far from the volcano (Nevado del Ruiz 1985, >20,000 dead). Both come from felsic, explosive, subduction-zone volcanoes.

Key ideas: hot · gas · collapse · fast · water · mud · felsic

Q7. Describe a Plinian eruption: what drives it and why is it so deadly?

A Plinian eruption is a sustained, gas-driven explosive eruption of felsic, viscous, volatile-rich magma (typically at subduction zones). Volatiles stay trapped until the magma nears the surface, then exsolve violently, fragmenting the magma and blasting a column of hot gas, ash and pumice 25–50 km high at ~600–700 m/s. Column collapse produces pyroclastic flows; ash falls widely; it can kill tens of thousands (Vesuvius AD 79, named for Pliny the Younger; Mt. St. Helens 1980).

Key ideas: felsic · gas · sustained · 25 · pyroclastic · vesuvius

Q8. What is a caldera eruption, and why are calderas like Campi Flegrei considered so dangerous?

A caldera forms when a very large eruption (often Plinian) empties the magma chamber and the volcano collapses into it; the collapse can drive out still more magma. Caldera eruptions are the largest (though rare). Campi Flegrei near Naples is more dangerous than famous Vesuvius because it has no obvious cone, could erupt from many vents, and 500,000+ people live inside it. Warning signs include ground uplift, earthquakes and changing gases.

Key ideas: empt · collapse · chamber · largest · naples · vent

Q9. Describe pahoehoe and a’a lava flows. What do mafic lava flows threaten?

Both are mafic (basaltic) lava flows typical of Hawaii. Pahoehoe is very runny and hardens with a smooth, ropy surface; a’a is slightly more viscous, forming thick, blocky, rubbly flows. Lava also forms tubes, lakes, and underwater pillows. These flows mainly destroy property (Kalapana, Hawaii 1990): they move slowly enough to walk away from, so they rarely kill.

Key ideas: mafic · ropy · blocky · property · slow

Q10. A volcano erupts in the Andes of Chile. Predict its magma type, eruption style and volcano shape, and explain your reasoning. Then do the same for a volcano on Réunion Island (a hotspot).

  • Chile (Andes): ocean–continent subduction (Nazca under South America) → water causes flux melting → magma rises through thick continental crust and melts felsic material → felsic, volatile-rich → explosive (Vulcanian/Plinian) → steep stratovolcano, with pyroclastic flows and lahars.
  • Réunion (hotspot): hot, dry plume → decompression melting → mafic → effusive (Hawaiian-style) lava flows and fire fountains → broad shield volcano.

Key ideas: subduction · felsic · explosive · stratovolcano · hotspot · mafic · shield · decompression

Q11. Why are large explosive eruptions sometimes deadly far beyond the volcano itself (e.g., Tambora 1815)?

Huge Plinian eruptions inject vast amounts of ash and gas high into the atmosphere. Ashfall destroys crops, causing famine (Tambora: ~10,000 killed directly by pyroclastic flows, ~100,000 more by famine), and volcanic gases/aerosols can cool the global climate (“the year without a summer,” 1816). Volcanic hazards can be global and indirect.

Key ideas: ash · crop · climate · atmosphere

Q12. A student sends the 🌋 emoji to describe Kilauea in Hawaii. What’s wrong?

The emoji shows a steep-sided, cone-shaped stratovolcano, the kind built by viscous, explosive felsic magma at subduction zones (Fuji, St. Helens). Hawaii is a hotspot with runny mafic lava that flows far and builds broad, gently sloping shield volcanoes. Wrong kind of volcano!

Key ideas: stratovolcano · steep · shield · mafic · felsic · hotspot

L1–3 · Energy sources & basic physics

  • What are the 3 categories of natural disasters and their energy sources?: Geological (earthquakes, volcanoes, tsunami) ← Earth’s interior heat; Meteorological (hurricanes, storms, floods) ← the Sun; Cosmological (asteroid impacts) ← planetary orbits (orbital KE + gravitational PE)
  • Earth’s total internal heat output: ~46 TW (4.6 × 10¹³ W). About 50% primordial heat + 50% radioactive decay.
  • Primordial heat: Heat left over from Earth’s formation: colliding bodies during accretion turned gravitational PE → KE → heat on impact.
  • Radiogenic heat: Heat from radioactive decay of U → Pb, Th → Pb, K → Ar; emitted α, β, γ particles are absorbed and become heat.
  • Solar constant & total solar power: 1,360 W/m²; total ≈ 170,000 TW, about 4,000× Earth’s internal heat.
  • Fate of incoming sunlight: ~30% reflected (albedo) · ~50% absorbed as heat · ~20% evaporates water · ~0.02% photosynthesis.
  • What powers the Sun?: Nuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium (proton–proton reaction), ignited when gravitational collapse heated the core above 10⁷ K.
  • Three lines of evidence for the Big Bang: 1. Dark night sky (finite universe & age) 2. Galactic redshift (Hubble/Doppler: universe expanding) 3. Cosmic microwave background (universe is 3 K, not 0 K: residual heat)
  • How do energy sources interact? (example): Tides: the Moon’s (and Sun’s) gravity raises bulges in oceans and the solid Earth; orbital/rotational energy goes into moving water and a little heat. On Io, tidal heating powers volcanoes.
  • Oil furnace: original energy source?: The Sun. Fossil fuels are buried organisms whose energy came from photosynthesis (the class trick question).
  • Differentiation: Early in Earth’s history (first tens of Myr) dense iron sank to form the core, leaving the mantle and crust on top.
  • F = ma; unit of force: Newton’s 2nd law. Newton (N) = kg·m/s². An apple weighs ~1 N.
  • Work; unit of work/energy: Work = force × distance moved = energy transferred by a force. Joule (J) = N·m = kg·m²/s².
  • Power; unit: Rate of energy transfer. Watt (W) = J/s. (1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J.)
  • Potential vs. kinetic energy: PE = stored energy (gravitational PE = mgh). KE = energy of motion (½mv²); heat is molecular KE. They interconvert; energy is conserved.
  • Sensible vs. latent heat: Sensible: changes temperature. Latent: changes phase (melting, evaporating) at constant temperature; released on freezing/condensing.
  • Heat capacity: Amount of heat needed to raise an object’s temperature by 1 degree (a sensible-heat property). Water’s is very high.
  • MKS units: Meters, kilograms, seconds: the units used in scientific calculations.
  • Precision rule: A result can’t be more precise than the least precise input (tsunami speed ≈ 230 m/s, not 232.282156).
  • Why does “Earth = rock sphere” underestimate Earth’s mass?: The core is iron (much denser than rock) and deep rock is compressed by pressure. (Estimate 3.6 × 10²⁴ vs. real 6 × 10²⁴ kg.)

L4 · Our mobile planet (convection, water, planets)

  • Thermal convection in one line: “Hot stuff rises, cold stuff falls.” Heated material expands → less dense → buoyant → rises; cooled material → denser → sinks.
  • Convection gives Earth two mobile layers: Mobile surface (mantle convection → plate tectonics) and mobile atmosphere (atmospheric circulation). This mobility drives disasters.
  • Mantle heat output vs. total: Total interior 46 TW; ~8 TW made in the crust, so ~38 TW comes out of the mantle, concentrated in up- and downwellings.
  • Speed of mantle convection: ~1–10 cm/yr, about as fast as fingernails grow.
  • Why does Earth spin create 3 atmospheric cells per hemisphere?: The Coriolis effect breaks the single equator-to-pole cell into the Hadley, Ferrel and Polar cells.
  • Water’s special energy properties: High heat capacity + high latent heat of melting and especially vaporization/condensation; ice is less dense than liquid water. Water “holds” a lot of energy.
  • Fraction of solar power taken up by water: ~23% of solar input goes into evaporating water. Condensation later releases it and powers storms and hurricanes.
  • Why is Earth more “mobile” than Mars, Mercury, the Moon?: Earth is the largest terrestrial planet, so it retains heat and its mantle still convects. Small bodies cooled quickly.
  • Venus vs. Earth: Same size, but too close to the Sun → runaway greenhouse (470 °C), lost its water → no plate tectonics(?), no life; spins slowly and backward → no cyclonic weather.
  • Mars vs. Earth: Too small and too cold: water frozen in polar caps and permafrost, no plate tectonics now (maybe in the past); spins like Earth → huge dust storms.
  • Habitable zone: Orbital distance where it’s neither too hot nor too cold for liquid water, which affects weather, plate tectonics and life.
  • Effects of Earth’s fast rotation: Coriolis effect → cyclonic weather, jet streams, ocean gyres/Gulf Stream; also core flow → magnetic field.

L5–6 · Plate tectonics, boundaries & evidence

  • Plate tectonics is the surface manifestation of…: Mantle convection. Plates are the cold top boundary layer of the convecting mantle.
  • Lithosphere: Crust + uppermost mantle. Cold → strong and stiff, resistant to flow; broken into plates.
  • Asthenosphere: Mantle beneath the lithosphere: solid but weaker, flows relatively easily.
  • Two observations proving the solid mantle can flow: Isostasy (it CAN flow) and post-glacial rebound (HOW EASILY it flows, i.e., its viscosity).
  • Post-glacial rebound: Land pushed down by ice-age ice sheets (Scandinavia, Canada) is still slowly rising thousands of years after the ice melted, like memory foam. The rate gives the mantle viscosity.
  • What drives plate motion?: Slab pull: old, cold, dense lithosphere sinks at subduction zones and drags the plate behind it. Ridges are passive.
  • Plate tectonic cycle duration; oldest seafloor: ~200–250 Myr; oldest seafloor ~200 Myr (vs. ~4 Byr continental rocks).
  • Three plate boundary types: Divergent (apart; ridges), convergent (together; subduction/collision), transform (slide past; San Andreas).
  • Three kinds of convergent boundary: Ocean–ocean (older, denser plate subducts; island arc), ocean–continent (oceanic plate subducts; continental arc), continent–continent (collision → mountains; no subduction).
  • Earthquake size by boundary: Divergent = smallest · Transform = middle · Convergent = largest (subduction zones: M9+).
  • 8 lines of evidence for plate tectonics: Continent shapes · magnetic stripes · paleomagnetism · hotspot tracks · earthquake/volcano distribution · deep earthquakes · seafloor age/depth/sediment · GPS satellite measurements.
  • Magnetic stripes: Bands of normal/reversed magnetization parallel to and symmetric about ridges, matching the reversal timescale → seafloor spreading (“smoking gun”).
  • Paleomagnetism: Rocks freeze in the field’s inclination → the latitude where they formed (not longitude). Dated rocks map continents’ past positions.
  • Hotspot track evidence: Volcanic islands get older away from the active hotspot (Hawaii → Midway → Emperor seamounts): the plate moves over a fixed plume.
  • Deep earthquakes (Wadati–Benioff zones): Quakes down to ~700 km along dipping zones under trenches. Only cold, brittle subducting slabs can break that deep: direct evidence of subduction.
  • Why does the ocean get deeper away from ridges?: Lithosphere cools, contracts, becomes denser with age → sinks lower by isostasy.
  • Why was Wegener rejected?: No mechanism: continents couldn’t plow through stiff rock. Fix: continents ride inside plates.
  • Fit of the continents: which contour?: The ~1,800 m depth contour (edge of the continental shelf = true edge of continental crust).
  • Why don’t continents subduct?: Continental crust is felsic, low density, buoyant: too light to sink into the mantle.

L7 · Viscosity, convection, rheology & isostasy

  • Viscosity: A fluid’s resistance to flow (“stickiness”). Units: pascal-seconds (Pa·s) = N·s/m².
  • Viscosities to remember: Air ~10⁻⁵ · water ~10⁻³ · corn syrup ~1 · molasses ~5 · peanut butter ~250 · Silly Putty ~10⁵ · mantle ~10²¹ Pa·s.
  • Fluid vs. liquid: The mantle is a fluid (it flows) but a solid, not a liquid. Never call the mantle liquid!
  • Why does a rock sink slower in syrup than water?: Gravity pulls it down; viscous drag resists, and drag grows with viscosity → lower terminal velocity in more viscous fluid.
  • What controls whether convection happens?: Bigger temperature difference (+), bigger size (+), thermal properties, higher viscosity (−).
  • Why does the super-viscous mantle still convect?: The enormous temperature difference (thousands of °C at the core vs. ~10 °C at the surface) and huge size overwhelm the high viscosity.
  • Lava lamp: where the analogy breaks: (1) two different materials (mantle = mostly the same rock); (2) no plates: the lamp’s top isn’t part of the convection.
  • Rheology: How materials deform.
  • Three deformation types (Silly Putty): Viscous (slow pull → flows; mantle convection) · Brittle (yank fast → snaps; lithosphere earthquakes) · Elastic (bounce → springs back; seismic waves).
  • Two controls on rheology: Strain rate (slow → viscous, fast → brittle/elastic) and temperature (cold → stiff/brittle, hot → flows). Temperature dominates in the mantle.
  • Isostasy: Less-dense crust “floats” on denser mantle, sinking until it displaces its own weight. Mountains have deep roots.
  • Iceberg: fraction below water: ~90% (920/1000 ≈ 92%) because ice is only slightly less dense than water. Fraction submerged = ρ_ice/ρ_water.
  • Crust and mantle densities: Continental crust ~2,700 kg/m³; mantle ~3,300 kg/m³; ice ~920; water ~1,000.

L8 · Earthquakes, faults & elastic rebound

  • Earthquake (definition): Shaking of rock as elastic waves pass through it, most commonly caused by sudden movement of rock across a fault.
  • Fault: A fracture in the Earth across which the two sides move relative to each other.
  • Hypocenter vs. epicenter: Hypocenter (focus): point on the fault where rupture starts, including depth. Epicenter: point on the surface directly above it.
  • Normal fault: Hanging wall moves down relative to footwall · extension · divergent boundaries (L’Aquila 2009).
  • Reverse / thrust fault: Hanging wall moves up relative to footwall · compression · convergent boundaries (Chile 1960, Alaska 1964, Sumatra 2004, Tohoku 2011).
  • Strike-slip fault: Blocks slide horizontally past each other · shear · transform boundaries (Haiti 2010, San Andreas = right-lateral).
  • Hanging wall vs. footwall: Hanging wall = block above an inclined fault; footwall = block below (your feet stand on it).
  • Right-lateral vs. left-lateral: Stand on one side, look across: if the far side moves to your right, it’s right-lateral (same answer from either side).
  • Bends in strike-slip faults: Push-together bend → compression → thrusting → mountains (Transverse Ranges); pull-apart bend → extension → normal faults → basins (Dead Sea).
  • Elastic rebound theory: Plate motion bends locked rocks elastically (stores strain) → stress exceeds fault strength → rupture → rocks snap back to an offset position → stored energy released as seismic waves.
  • Foreshock / mainshock / aftershock: Mainshock = largest; foreshocks before; aftershocks after. You only know a foreshock was a foreshock after the mainshock, so foreshocks can’t predict quakes.
  • Is an earthquake a point?: No. Rupture starts at the hypocenter and spreads over a large fault area (Sumatra 2004: ~1,200 km in ~7–8 min).
  • Seismometer: A mass on a spring in a frame bolted to the ground; the mass’s inertia keeps it still while the ground moves; records 3 components (vertical, N–S, E–W).

L8–9, 11 · Seismic waves, seismograms & location

  • Body waves vs. surface waves: Body (P, S): travel through Earth; faster; higher frequency. Surface (Love, Rayleigh): travel along the surface; slower; lower frequency, larger amplitude, more damaging.
  • P wave: Primary · body · compressional/longitudinal (particle motion parallel to travel, like sound or a pushed Slinky) · fastest, arrives first (~5 km/s crust) · travels through solids and liquids.
  • S wave: Secondary · body · shear/transverse (motion perpendicular to travel, like a shaken rope) · second (~3 km/s) · cannot travel through liquids (no rigidity).
  • Love wave: Surface · horizontal side-to-side shaking perpendicular to travel · most damaging to buildings.
  • Rayleigh wave: Surface · rolling elliptical motion (up-down + back-forth, retrograde), like ocean swells · mostly vertical · less damaging than Love.
  • Why are all seismic waves “elastic waves”?: Rock deforms, stores elastic potential energy, springs back, and pushes the next bit: a continuous PE ↔︎ KE exchange (Slinky, human-chain demo).
  • Wave terms: Amplitude = displacement size · wavelength = distance between crests · period = time between crests · frequency = waves per second = 1/period.
  • Order of arrival on a seismogram: P → S → Love → Rayleigh. P is small and high-frequency; surface waves are big and long-period.
  • S–P time: The delay between P and S arrivals grows with distance → tells how far away the quake is.
  • How many stations to locate a quake?: Three (trilateration): 1 → circle, 2 → two possible points, 3 → one epicenter.
  • Lightning–thunder analogy: Light ≈ instant, sound ≈ 0.2 mi/s, so ~5 s per mile. Same idea as S–P time, but P is only ~2× faster than S.
  • Why are P waves faster than S waves?: P waves are resisted by both compression and shear stiffness; S waves only by rigidity (shear stiffness). Speed ≈ √(stiffness/density).

L9–10 · Magnitude, intensity & building damage

  • Magnitude: The size/energy of an earthquake: one value per quake. All scales are logarithmic.
  • +1 magnitude means…: 10× the wave amplitude (shaking) and ~32× the energy. M9 vs M7: 100× amplitude, ~1,000× energy.
  • Richter magnitude: First scale: log of peak amplitude on a seismogram, corrected for distance. Underestimates large/distant quakes; seismologists rarely use it now.
  • Moment magnitude (Mw): Based on seismic moment = rock rigidity × rupture area × slip. Most accurate; used for big quakes.
  • Rupture length vs. magnitude: M4 ≈ 100 m · M5 ≈ 1 km · M6 ≈ 10 km · M7 ≈ 100 km · M8+ ≈ 1,000+ km. “Large earthquakes mean large faults.”
  • Largest earthquake ever recorded: M9.5, Chile 1960. There is no M10: no fault is long enough.
  • Mercalli intensity: Shaking at a particular place (I–XII). Varies across locations; depends on magnitude, distance, duration, local geology.
  • Why vertical shaking is relatively safe: Buildings are designed to carry vertical loads (gravity), not sideways forces.
  • Natural sway periods: 1-story house ≈ 0.1 s; 30-story building ≈ 3 s. Flexible (wood, steel) → longer; stiff (brick, concrete) → shorter.
  • Resonance: When the wave period matches a structure’s natural sway period, shaking is amplified: a common cause of collapse.
  • Site amplification: Soft sediment, mud, fill and lake beds amplify shaking: waves slow down so amplitude grows (energy conservation), and basins resonate.
  • High vs. low frequency waves and damage: High-frequency: damage near the epicenter, die out quickly. Low-frequency (long-period): travel far, resonate with tall buildings and bridges.
  • Rupture size → wave frequency: Short rupture/short duration → high frequency; long rupture/long duration → low frequency.
  • Depth and damage: Shallow quakes generate strong surface waves (more damage); deep quakes don’t.

L11 · Stick-slip, slider block & seismic gaps

  • Stick-slip behavior: A high-friction fault sticks while stress builds, then slips suddenly (earthquake), then sticks again, repeating as the earthquake cycle.
  • Slider-block demo: what represents what?: Steady hand = plate motion · bungee/rubber band = elastic crust (stores strain) · brick on sandpaper = fault held by friction · lurch = earthquake.
  • Two-brick demo shows…: First brick slips repeatedly, each slip loads the second; the locked second brick = a seismic gap; it slips when enough stress accumulates.
  • Seismic gap: A fault segment that hasn’t ruptured in a long time while its neighbors have: still loaded, so the likely site of the next big quake (where, not when).
  • Fault creep: Low-friction segments slide steadily with small quakes instead of sticking (central San Andreas creeping section).
  • Seismic gap examples: Shumagin Gap (Alaska) · North Anatolian Fault next to Istanbul · Cascadia · Michoacán gap (filled 1985) · locked San Andreas segments.
  • North Anatolian Fault sequence: Ruptured segment by segment marching westward (1939 → 1999 Izmit): stress transfer to the neighbor; the remaining gap is near Istanbul.
  • Fridge analogy: Push an old fridge: nothing, nothing… then it suddenly lurches once you overcome friction. That’s stick-slip.

L12 · Volcanic settings & why the mantle melts

  • Three settings for almost all volcanism: 1. Divergent boundaries (mid-ocean ridges) 2. Convergent boundaries (subduction zones) 3. Hotspots (mantle plumes).
  • Volcanism at transform boundaries?: None. Horizontal sliding only: no upwelling (no decompression) and no water (no flux melting).
  • Mid-ocean ridge volcanism: Decompression melting of passively upwelling shallow mantle · mafic · underwater pillow basalts · ~80% of Earth’s magma · makes oceanic crust (~7 km) · least dangerous.
  • Pillow basalts: Mafic lava erupted underwater, quenched quickly by cold seawater into pillow-shaped blobs (also behind the Target in Meriden, CT: Pangaea breakup ~200 Myr).
  • Hotspot volcanism: Hot, buoyant solid plume from the deep mantle → decompression melting that starts deeper → more melt → islands (isostasy) · mafic · gentle.
  • Subduction-zone volcanism: Slab releases water into the mantle wedge → dehydration/flux melting → wet, felsic, volatile-rich magma → most explosive · volcanic arcs.
  • Island arc vs. continental arc: Island arc: ocean under ocean (Japan, Aleutians, Philippines). Continental arc: ocean under continent (Cascades, Andes).
  • Melting temperature vs. pressure: Melting T increases with pressure/depth: high pressure makes it harder for atoms to break bonds and form a liquid.
  • Decompression (pressure-release) melting: Hot mantle rises, pressure drops, the melting point drops below its temperature → melts without adding heat (ridges, hotspots).
  • Dehydration (flux, wet) melting: Water from the slab lowers the melting temperature of the mantle wedge → it melts without heating. Like salt on an icy sidewalk.
  • Are subduction zones hotter than average mantle?: No, colder (cold slabs = ice cubes in a drink). Melting is due to water, not heating.
  • Volcanoes as mantle samples: Ridge lavas sample the shallow mantle; hotspot lavas sample the deep mantle (different trace-element chemistry).
  • Number of hotspots; biggest: ~100–120; biggest: Hawaii and Iceland (Iceland = ridge + plume).

L13 · Silicates, Bowen, felsic vs mafic, viscosity, volatiles

  • Magma vs. lava: Magma = molten rock below the surface; lava = molten rock erupted at the surface. Same stuff, different location.
  • Two most abundant elements in the crust: Oxygen (~half) and silicon. 8 elements make ~98% of the crust.
  • Silicate tetrahedron: One Si bonded to four O (SiO₄) in a tetrahedron. Corner oxygens still want a bond, so tetrahedra link into chains and sheets.
  • Felsic: Feldspar + silica: high silica (>65%), light elements, low density, low melting T, high viscosity, traps gas; continental crust; granite/rhyolite.
  • Mafic: Magnesium + Fe (iron): low silica, heavy elements, high density, high melting T, low viscosity; oceanic crust; basalt/gabbro.
  • Bowen’s reaction series (cooling): Mafic minerals crystallize first (highest T: olivine, pyroxene) → felsic last (lowest T: feldspar, mica, quartz).
  • Bowen’s reaction series (heating): Felsic minerals melt first (lowest T) → mafic minerals melt last. (Class poll answer: felsic!)
  • Three rock types: Igneous (frozen magma/lava) · sedimentary (eroded grains deposited & cemented) · metamorphic (changed by heat/pressure without melting).
  • Intrusive vs. extrusive igneous: Intrusive/plutonic: cools slowly underground → coarse crystals (granite). Extrusive/volcanic: cools fast at surface → fine grains (basalt).
  • Two key magma properties: Viscosity (stickiness) and volatile content (dissolved H₂O and CO₂).
  • Three controls on magma viscosity: Temperature (hotter → runnier) · silica content (more → stickier) · crystal content (more → stickier).
  • Mafic vs. felsic viscosity analogy: Mafic = melted ice cream (runny); felsic = toothpaste (sticky).
  • Volatiles and pressure: Gas solubility rises with pressure. As magma rises, pressure drops, so gas exsolves into bubbles (like opening a shaken soda).
  • Which magma traps volatiles?: Felsic (viscous): bubbles get stuck → pressure builds → explosion. Mafic lets gas escape.
  • Where do magma volatiles come from?: Mostly subducting slabs: water from seawater-altered minerals and CO₂ from carbonate sediments.
  • Why do continents stand above sea level?: Continental crust is felsic, so it is less dense and floats higher (isostasy).
  • Mineral vs. rock: Mineral: naturally occurring, inorganic, crystalline solid. Rock: aggregate of one or more minerals.

L14 · Eruption styles, flows & volcano types

  • Non-explosive eruption styles (mafic): Hawaiian/Icelandic: fire fountains then basalt lava flows · Strombolian: short bursts, tens to ~100 m.
  • Explosive eruption styles (felsic): Vulcanian (short blasts, bombs, 5–10 km) → Plinian (sustained gas-driven column, 25–50 km, ~600–700 m/s) → caldera (chamber collapse; the biggest).
  • Plinian eruption: Named for Pliny the Younger (Vesuvius AD 79). Sustained, gas-driven column of ash and pumice 25–50 km high; felsic, volatile-rich, subduction zone (Mt. St. Helens 1980).
  • Caldera: After a huge eruption drains the magma chamber, the volcano collapses into it, often driving out more magma (Yellowstone, Campi Flegrei).
  • Pahoehoe vs. a’a: Both mafic lava flows. Pahoehoe: smooth, ropy, runny. A’a: thick, blocky/rubbly, slightly more viscous.
  • Pyroclastic flow: Ground-hugging avalanche of hot gas, ash and rock from a collapsing eruption column; ~300 km/h; can’t outrun it (Mt. Pelée 1902: ~30,000 dead in ~10 s).
  • Lahar: Volcanic mudflow: ash + water (rain, rivers, melted snow and ice) → fast “wet cement” down valleys (~50 km/h) (Nevado del Ruiz 1985: >20,000 dead).
  • The two big volcanic killers: Pyroclastic flows and lahars: both from felsic, explosive, subduction-zone volcanoes.
  • Shield volcano: Broad, gentle slopes · mafic, runny, low-volatile, high-volume lava · hotspots (Hawaii: Mauna Loa, Mauna Kea, Kilauea).
  • Stratovolcano (= composite volcano): Steep, layered cone of viscous lava + ash + pyroclastics + lahars · felsic · subduction zones (Fuji, St. Helens, Mayon, Vesuvius).
  • The three V’s: Viscosity, Volatiles, Volume control eruption style and volcano shape (focus on viscosity and volatiles).
  • The volcano emoji 🌋 is a…: Stratovolcano, not Hawaii! Hawaii is a shield volcano.
  • Campi Flegrei lesson (reading): A caldera near Naples is a bigger danger than famous Vesuvius: no cone, many possible vents, huge population. Know which hazard applies where.
  • Hazard from Hawaiian eruptions: Mostly property (lava buries roads and towns like Kalapana 1990); you can walk away. Risks: reckless tourists, toxic gas at ocean entries.
  • Tambora 1815 lesson: Huge Plinian eruption → pyroclastic flows (~10,000 dead) + famine (~100,000) + global cooling (“year without a summer,” 1816).
